# Future issue in slavery law: original cases and competing dispositions

## Research question

The client's account of settler social reproduction contains more than a demographic proportion of births to captive mothers. It asks whether *the capacity to produce new people* was converted into a material engine for a polity that denied those people their own freedom and property. This note replaces the earlier abstract-only citation to [Alphonso (2023)](../../../../bibliographies/alphonso2023.md) with **specific cases in which particular courts allocated real and anticipated children among slaveholding beneficiaries**, and preserves one contrary outcome where a petitioner secured his freedom.

The evidence does NOT license the inverse leap: ownership of future issue is not direct proof that every conception was intentionally directed. The independent [Everett 1936 retrospective account](../../../../bibliographies/everett-randolph1936.md) documents actual coercion in one named case, but not how many people were treated that way across the South.

## Original-source admission and study design

Four full appellate judicial reports and two historical slaveholder letters were independently read in public transcriptions. One Kentucky opinion was encountered only through published citations and a 2023 preprint. The case corpus is **purposive, selected for variation in outcome**: transfer to a remainderman, transfer to a direct legatee, court-ordered freedom, and maternal emancipation accompanied by continuing child slavery. It is not a denominator for the fraction of all antebellum slave-law cases deciding similarly. A court's report names the questions it deemed pertinent to property law; it often suppresses mothers' voices, their family histories, and the meaning of a child's birth beyond law's claimed title.

The seven source records produce **21 fact-specific observations**, each with a source locator, statement, explicit reading grade and rejected interpretations. The source-owned [case evidence register](../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-10-17-antebellum-property-law-case-register.json) preserves their metadata and document-level limitations. Distinct children in three unrelated lawsuits must not be pooled into a prevalence estimate: they are reported survivors or children specifically disputed, not independent fertility-observation samples.

## What the decisions actually establish

**Legal condition versus owner.** *Smith v Milman* is strongest here. Delaware's court recognized that the maternal-condition rule answers free-or-enslaved status at birth, but said it could not itself decide **which of multiple claimants had legal title**. Stephen's manumission succeeded because the person whose title the court accepted had executed the relevant deed. This directly qualifies any assertion that a child's ownership “automatically followed its mother's owner” in every American jurisdiction. The mother's status and the child's title are distinct legal predicates.

**Temporal ownership partitions.** *Covington* and *Nelson* apply North Carolina will and remainder law differently because the relevant drafts and times differed. Binah's children born during the widow's life interest were included in the later beneficiary's gift even though the executor had claimed them. Leah's three children were born *after* James Nelson's death and so excluded by the separate gift's temporal limitation; instead they went with Leah's main bequest to Elizabeth. In each case the legal allocation of actual children depended on the reading of documents written *before* the children were born.

**Emancipation is temporal, not retroactive.** *Johnson* distinguishes an enslaved mother's freedom that became effective at widow's death from freedom declared to exist earlier but subject to deferred enjoyment. Because Lytha's children were born **before** her emancipation took effect, they remained legally enslaved even after she became free. The decision allocated property claims over them to Margaret Johnson's estate, not to Lytha. Without independent life history, the record reveals a state-created legal separation of family members, not evidence they consented to it.

**Capitalizing possible births.** Alphonso's unreviewed preprint attributes to *Banks* an early instance of independently conveyed rights to children not yet conceived. The current record cannot verify its exact holding because the original opinion remains unrecovered. Even if that rule is later confirmed, it is distinct from the physical birth of a child or actual intended forced sexual conduct. The assignment is a **claim to prospective issue** in property law: legal imagination of capital preceding the possible person's existence.

**Fungibility of labor versus unborn capital.** Jefferson's two letters make the investment framing explicit without needing to infer it from legal language. His 1819 letter expresses the expected net value of slave children surviving infancy, while his June 30, 1820 letter states preference for a proposed mixed group of enslaved people over a smaller group of male workers, precisely because he expected women's children to add future property. A statement of intended estate practice is not verified implementation, and it can neither quantify births nor turn enslaved women into voluntary producers of slaveholders' wealth.

## Source-critical checks and contradictions

1. **Court case selectivity.** Reported opinions disproportionately preserve disputes that property owners or free petitioners pursued to appellate judgment. Ordinary births and relationships among enslaved communities are missing. No historical base rate can be inferred from seven selected records.
2. **Race, status, and jurisdiction.** [Wulf (2023)](../../../../bibliographies/wulf2023.md) traces nonuniform colony-by-colony enactment of the maternal-condition principle. A Virginia statute cannot stand in for Kentucky or Delaware legal title doctrine. Recognizing this does not imply slavery was voluntary.
3. **Legal claim versus real event.** A clause assigning future increase may never correspond to a birth. An enslaved child born in an owner's house may not have been conceived at the owner's direction. Compelled pairing is separately supported by specific oral and documentary testimony, such as the [Everett case](../../../../bibliographies/everett-randolph1936.md).
4. **Money is category-specific.** The 1849 *Nelson* amount describes maintenance of Leah *and family*; the 1842 *Covington* amount concerns a proposed estate compromise. No price-per-infant estimand exists in these documents. Neither number is a population-level income estimate.
5. **The countercase is retained.** Stephen's freedom in *Smith* is contrary evidence to automatic perpetuation of slaveholding title. Lytha's own later freedom beside her children's continued captivity demonstrates legal differentiation *within a single family*.
6. **Authorship of source.** The mother may be named without being heard. Legal opinions reveal enslavers' claims and the court's authorized resolution; they do not adjudicate the ethical legitimacy of enslaving her or her children.

## The next scientific dataset that would support an actual quantitative economic paper

An executable larger case study would need original archival probate/freedom/manumission materials linked by named mother and child, with a governed sampling frame: region + date range + complete docket volumes, not only keyword hits. The strongest measurable estimands are **(i) rate of separate conveyance of future issue among all eligible wills/deeds**, **(ii) probability and determinants of remainder- versus life-tenant outcomes**, **(iii) observable monetary appraisal of prospective issue distinct from observed maintenance and labor earnings**, and **(iv) success of freedom petitions under different maternal-status/manumission timings**. These require denominators and source metadata not present in this dossier. Do not construct imaginary zero counts for missing documents.

For the client's *first* conjecture about white reproductive exhaustion, this property dataset supplies a different but necessary mechanism: a slaveholding order may reproduce wealth from Black captive births without reproducing all its wealth through its own children. Whether an unattainable ideal of whiteness induced the depletion of white people's actual reproductive conditions is a distinct **policy-choice and cohort-outcome** study. That cannot be established by this case corpus alone.
