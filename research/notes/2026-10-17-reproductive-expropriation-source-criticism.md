# Coerced captive reproduction, hereditary status, future issue, and trade

The client's historical question about an industrially organized regime of captive births cannot be answered by dismissing all directed breeding as myth, nor by labeling every captive birth a purposeful breeding event. **The evidence concerns several materially different processes that must be measured separately.** The quantitative birth share describes one of them, and cannot stand in for the others.

## Four distinct historical questions

| Process | Relevant original evidence | What it does not directly show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Directed coerced sexual/reproductive arrangements | Testimony and correspondence recording specific acts, enslavers directing pairings, reproductive exploitation of women and girls, and the use of men called “stock men” | Population prevalence of such acts without a defined sampling frame |
| Inherited captive condition | Virginia Act XII, December 1662: child legally held bond or free by maternal condition | That a specific pregnancy was intentionally arranged or consensual |
| Anticipated future issue as capital | Enslavers' wills, deeds, probate, litigation, chattel-price records treating as property the potential children of an enslaved woman | A future child was conceived, born, survived, appraised accurately, or sold |
| Domestic trade and interregional transfer | Records of selling and transporting already living persons from Upper to Lower South, including children | An additional birth, much less the circumstances of conception |

**Aisha Djeliid's 2024 peer-reviewed article directly addresses the first process**, including coercion, “stock men”, and forced reproductive relationships. Earlier research failed by discussing a breeding-plantation hypothesis without initially consulting this obvious relevant article. The source abstract and first-page publication details have been inspected; the original case studies must be read and logged separately before quoting prevalence or a particular victim's testimony. [Djeliid 2024](../../../../bibliographies/djeliid2024.md).

**Virginia's original 1662 statute** demonstrates the second process and specifically addresses Englishmen fathering children with enslaved Black women. [Virginia 1662](../../../../bibliographies/virginia-assembly1662.md). **Alphonso's 2023 preprint** develops the third from slavery's property law, including rights in unborn future increase; it remains a preprint pending independent inspection of the underlying judicial opinions. [Alphonso 2023](../../../../bibliographies/alphonso2023.md). The fourth process includes the transfer of more than one million enslaved persons from Upper to Lower South as described in [Virginia Humanities' historical synthesis](../../../../bibliographies/virginia-domestic-slave-trade.md), but that count is of transported persons, not births.

The kinship and reproductive agency of enslaved persons cannot be erased in order to model an imaginary factory. [Hortense Spillers (1987)](../../../../bibliographies/spillers1987.md) and [Jennifer L. Morgan (2021)](../../../../bibliographies/morgan2021.md) frame how racialized law violently redefined family and kinship, without thereby implying the enslaved were incapable of maintaining families, relationships, desire, resistance and care.

## An executed primary-source extraction: Sam and Louisa Everett, recorded 1936

The first historical case is now recovered from the original interview's catalogued identity rather than the secondary article's abstract. The University of South Florida archives `SFS0021855_00001` as **“Interview, Sam Everett, Louisa Everett, and Pearl Randolph, Slave Interview, October 8, 1936,”** six pages; Pearl Randolph was interviewer and John A. Simms editor. [USF original catalogue](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/formerly_enslaved_narratives/5/) and [1936 FWP Florida transcription](https://www.genealogytrails.com/fla/SlaveNarratives.htm), from the “SAM AND LOUISA EVERETT” header through its concluding reference. The Library of Congress retrospective collection is *Slave Narratives*, Volume III, Florida. The USF scan itself was catalogued and its metadata checked; the wording below was checked through the FWP transcription, not a direct image-to-text collation of USF's six-page PDF.

**Specific material observation (one account; not a frequency):** An interviewer/editor narrative reports that the enslaver called “Big Jim” McClain selected enslaved people he considered likely to have healthy children, compelled relationships across existing family unions, and compelled sexual relations in his presence. **Louisa's directly attributed first-person recollection** independently describes the enslaver ordering her and Sam into a sexual relationship while carrying a whip, followed by their having children. The claim that over one hundred people lived on the plantation is **the interviewer/editor's account**, not an independently verified plantation roster or a numerator of coerced conceptions. The interview also recounts care, constrained child-rearing arrangements, and Sam and Louisa continuing a household after emancipation; it does not provide the number or exact birth dates of children conceived as a consequence of the intervention.

The public historical transcription has two voices with different evidentiary status: Louisa's quoted words (still rendered through 1930s transcription, editing and retrospective recollection) and the field worker/editor's surrounding third-person narrative. A rape/coercion claim need not be discarded because survivors later sustained affection or kinship. Xavier Reader's [2023 original peer-reviewed article](../../../../bibliographies/reader2023.md), *Journal of American Studies* 57(4), p. 484, analyzes this same relationship as an example where coercion and later emotional attachment were not mutually exclusive. **Neither later care nor later birth demonstrates consent to an imposed sexual act.**

**Provenance conflict:** Aisha Djeliid's [2024 article](../../../../bibliographies/djeliid2024.md), opening paragraph, dates Louisa's recollection to *1937*. The USF catalog and FWP document header date the Everett interview to **October 8, 1936**. Preserve both and use the original interview date provisionally. The article's other cases have not been extracted; no sample-size or prevalence inference is drawn from this one case. The [structured case extract](../../../../../../../../data/records/2026-10-17-everett-directed-reproduction-case-extract.json) retains observation rows by speaker/mediation level and their falsifiable limits.

**Interrogation against the client's two-layer theory:** The Everett testimony gives positive *case evidence* for purposeful compelled reproduction under a slaveholding property regime. It is not demographic evidence for the share of births into slavery, and it cannot by itself show any population-wide decline among persons classified white. It is precisely the sort of primary linkage required before calling any birth “industrially directed.”

## Proposed original research assays

**C1: Directed coercion.** **Partly executed:** the Louisa/Sam Everett 1936 interview is now independently source-located as one case and retained with voice attribution and non-prevalence limits. Extract every further specifically attributable example in Djeliid's full article to an evidence table: date, place, first-person account or enslaver-authored source, method of control, age of victim if recoverable, and whether an actual birth followed. Authorial interpretation and the original speaker's evidence must remain distinct. A case series does not automatically estimate prevalence: legal and property archives may disproportionately preserve owners' accounts.

**C2: Capitalization of possible descendants.** Retrieve the original antebellum cases cited by Alphonso. For each, distinguish a litigant's demand, a court's holding, a dissent, the period/jurisdiction, and whether future issue was valued separately from the woman herself. A property doctrine does not mean that all pregnancies were directed by an enslaver.

**C3: Economic production.** Find appraisals and price/sale records in which prospective fertility or already born children were explicitly priced; compare against age, labor value, region, marriage/family separation, and demand shocks. The independent outcome is an economic price or asset claim, not an infant count.

**C4: Civic appropriation.** Distinguish constitutional three-fifths apportionment before emancipation from later full-count disfranchisement. Both can permit a governing population to derive representation from persons denied political power, but their legal mechanisms differ. Consult [Constitution 1787](../../../../bibliographies/constitution1787.md) and [Olson 2025](../../../../bibliographies/olson2025.md). Calculating an additional House seat requires a real apportionment counterfactual.

**C5: Birth-share denominator.** Retain the modeled legal-status numerator from [Hacker 2020](../../../../bibliographies/hacker2020.md). Independently record historical boundaries, mothers' status, all-birth denominators, and census exclusions rather than relabeling every modeled captive birth an industrial operation. The current figure, approximately one in six among the historically counted U.S. births, is a bounded reconstruction, not an estimate of directed breeding.

## Source receiving and ethical limits

Publishable research now holds the original Everett transcript for one case, but still requires the complete *other* cases and records behind Djeliid; the primary cases behind Alphonso; archival statements showing specific coercion or prospective property claims; and an explicit selection/exclusion rule for a case corpus. Name evidence unavailable as unavailable. Do not attribute a survivor's intentions, consent or kinship choices solely from an enslaver's property record. Do not convert “industrial” from a metaphor for law's distributed expropriation into an unsupported specific technology.
