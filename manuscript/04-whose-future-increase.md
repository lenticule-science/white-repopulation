# Whose Future Increase? Reproductive Property, Jurisdiction, and Freedom in Antebellum Slave Law

**Research status (17 October 2026).** Working article for hypothetical submission to *Law and History Review*. The client's authorship and permission to publish have not been determined. This is original comparative extraction from four publicly available historical court opinions plus two slaveholder letters. Original reporter page images, the underlying wills, and one further cited Kentucky court opinion still require recovery. Nothing here is presented as peer-reviewed or as authorized publication.

## Abstract

A newborn's inheritance of enslaved status under maternal-status law did not necessarily settle which enslaver claimed legal title to that child. This study compares four published judicial opinions from North Carolina, Delaware and Kentucky (1839–1849), a fifth Kentucky case available only through secondary citation, and two letters by Thomas Jefferson (1819–1820). *Covington v. McEntire* allocated four living children of Binah to a remainderman after a life tenancy; *Nelson v. Nelson* awarded three post-testator children of Leah to the beneficiary holding title to their mother; *Smith v. Milman* distinguished maternal status from ownership and granted the freedom petition of Stephen; and *Johnson's Administrator v. Johnson's Heirs* liberated Lytha but left her seven children, born before emancipation, enslaved. Judicial calculations assigned costs of maintaining mothers and children to the holders of future gains, while Jefferson contrasted women's anticipated reproductive capital with men's immediately expended labor. The resulting regime was not a single universal law of ownership and cannot be equated with proof that all pregnancies were deliberately compelled. The findings show juridical mechanisms by which anticipated children could be treated as property while rights of actual mothers and children remained restricted, disputed or, occasionally, vindicated through freedom petitions.

## Introduction: biological birth, status, title, and futurity are different objects

What does a future birth produce under hereditary slavery? A person born to a mother held enslaved might legally inherit her captive condition. But the birth did not mechanically identify who owned the child. Wills divided the mother's immediate services and her successors' property rights; deeds attempted to convey unconceived future descendants; manumission instruments could change the mother's status at one date without changing her children's prior status. Courts then translated claims upon an entirely uncertain reproductive future into disputes over identifiable people.

That distinction matters to the client's commission on the materiality of settler social reproduction. The state could treat children of an excluded people as increased labor supply, assignable inheritance, or legal assets, thereby reproducing the wealth of those recognized as entitled to rule. This does not mean each particular child was born as the result of a specialized enslaver-directed pairing, nor that the reproduction of white-classified households collapsed. It means **the reproduction of slaveholding property was not identical to the reproduction of the slaveholding families' own children**. Making that relationship empirical requires distinguishing the operation of law from the biology and desires of the people subjected to it.

The closest historical literature already understands this. Jennifer L. Morgan's *Reckoning with Slavery* (2021) connects reproduction, gender and racial capitalism; Hortense J. Spillers (1987) examines slavery's violence toward kinship recognition. Gwendoline Alphonso (2023), in an unreviewed APSA preprint whose full text was read in this inquiry, analyzes “future increase” as a separate property interest and reports a keyword-sourced group of 212 legal cases from NexisUNI, nearly four-fifths between 1830 and 1860. **That search has not been independently replicated**, and its results are neither an exhaustive universe nor a prevalence estimate. The contribution attempted here is not to rediscover prospective issue as property. It is to recover, compare and qualify court rulings with *different legal outcomes*, including a freedom petition, and to make their specific claims reusable as independent source-located research observations.

## Material, selection and method

The evidence is a purposive collection, not a random sample: four complete nineteenth-century reported decisions read in public report transcriptions; one reported decision for which only index and later citations were recovered; and two contemporary enslaver-authored letters. The four original opinions are [Covington v. McEntire, 37 N.C. 316 (1842)](../../../../bibliographies/case-covington-mcentire1842.md), [Nelson v. Nelson, 41 N.C. 409 (1849)](../../../../bibliographies/case-nelson-nelson1849.md), [Smith v. Milman, 2 Del. 497 (1839)](../../../../bibliographies/case-smith-milman1839.md), and [Johnson's Administrator v. Johnson's Heirs, 47 Ky. 470 (1848)](../../../../bibliographies/case-johnson-johnson1848.md). [Banks' Administrator v. Marksberry, 13 Ky. 275 (1823)](../../../../bibliographies/case-banks-marksberry1823.md) is retained as a **secondary reconstruction**, not falsely represented as an independently read original.

For each case the dataset separates: captive people named; testator or claimants; whether the mother was enslaved when a child was born; legal question of status versus title; wording and temporal boundary of bequest; any specified living issue; amount and scope of any monetary claim; decision's result; and source limitations. There are four primary court opinions, one secondary legal report and two letters, generating 21 narrowly stated observations. Named children are not counted as independent random samples. The corpus overrepresents disputes reaching appellate courts and published in law reporters and has severe archival asymmetry in whose speech is recorded.

Karin Wulf's (2023) historical account independently warns against treating Virginia's 1662 statute as a uniformly enacted legal rule across colonial British America. Maternal status could be implemented via statute in particular colonies, in other places via custom or court doctrines, and the later assignment of ownership was jurisdictionally contested. The distinctions tested here belong to historically specified law, not an imaginary biologically fixed race.

## Finding I. North Carolina divided reproductive title across time

In *Covington v. McEntire* (37 N.C. 316, 317–320), William McEntire's 1832 will held **Binah** in a life interest under his widow Rebecca and then assigned Binah to his daughter **Rachel Covington**. At Rebecca's death in 1842, Binah had **four children then living** who had been born during the widow's tenancy. Executor Prior McEntire claimed those children under the residuary estate. A proposed division discussed one named child, **Catharine**, and $55 as the plaintiffs' share if that theory prevailed. The amount was an offered distribution component, **not** an observed sale price for a child.

Gaston, J., applying precedent of his North Carolina court, treated issue born during the life estate as part of the remainder gift unless the will specifically withheld that increase. He affirmed Rachel's claim to Binah and the children. The court expressly rejected an inference that different, more elaborate wording concerning another enslaved woman, Nan, diminished the gift of Binah's issue. The lawsuit thus reveals two attempted temporal divisions of ownership: current labor and its expected future human descendants.

The four living children are a fact about that litigation. They are not Binah's complete birth history or proof that the claimants orchestrated conception. The court's own framework treated persons' relationships to one another as a dispute over the extent of other people's testamentary gifts. Binah's account of her family is absent.

The court in *Nelson v. Nelson* (41 N.C. 409, especially 410–412, 416–418) dealt with a different textual problem. James Nelson's 1843 will gave **Leah and baby Anderson** to **Elizabeth Nelson**, but another clause proposed dividing any additional increase of Leah among three daughters. After the testator's January 1844 death, **three more children** were born to Leah. Ruffin, C.J., held that the separate future-increase gift concerned possible births *before* the testator's death; the three post-death children passed to Elizabeth under the original bequest.

The court also reported **$329.75 in costs of maintaining Leah and her family**, requiring maintenance expenses to be borne alongside legal claims to associated income or future issue. That amount was not solely maternity costs, not a sale price, and not a universal price of childrearing. The legal argument nevertheless demonstrates the treatment of future persons as an expected return to a property owner for expenditures on a woman's pregnancy and her children's survival. Neither the legal category “increase” nor the ledger captures Leah's own reproductive choices.

## Finding II. Delaware separated inherited status from ownership and recognized a freedom claim

In *Smith v. Milman* (2 Del. 497, 498–499), the petitioner **Stephen**, son of **Hessy**, sought freedom. A contested testamentary chain raised whether his ownership had passed with his mother in a life estate or to another person. The Delaware court distinguished a child's inherited free-or-enslaved **condition** from the identity of the person claiming **title**. It criticized the assumption, used by courts elsewhere, that *partus sequitur ventrem* compelled title to children to follow whichever person held a remainder in their mother's labor. The court's alternative factual analyses left Stephen's birth order uncertain but agreed that Ann Smith had the power to manumit him. The resulting order granted **Stephen's freedom**.

This is an analytically consequential countercase, not a benign exception that makes the regime equitable. The court used the slaveholding property doctrine itself to recognize a person who could claim freedom through a valid manumission instrument. A uniform model predicting the same remainder-rule result in Delaware and North Carolina is falsified by the expressed reasoning of the original reports. Equally, a triumphalist story that the court stood for freedom over slavery would misstate its ownership premises.

The contrast should not be erased by selecting only property-consolidating decisions. The legal apparatus made children into contested assets, but where title claims failed and manumission applied, one person's freedom could be recognized. The result proves the need to preserve both positive and negative judgments in any larger case corpus.

## Finding III. Kentucky separated a mother's emancipation from seven children's bondage

*Johnson's Administrator v. Johnson's Heirs* (47 Ky. 470, 470–474) asks an even more painful question: what happens to children born while their mother is temporarily enslaved and anticipating eventual freedom? Job Johnson's will held **Lytha** in a life estate with widow **Margaret Johnson**, providing that she should become free when Margaret died, or upon attaining 21 if the widow died earlier. Margaret lived past Lytha's majority. **Seven children** were born to Lytha before Margaret died.

The court held Lytha free after Margaret's death, but her children had been born while she was enslaved and hence remained enslaved. The court ruled the seven were claimable by Margaret's estate rather than by Job Johnson's heirs, because he had retained no remainder in Lytha. The children were not awarded to Lytha herself. This result requires separating at least three historical dates: a testator's promise, a mother's actual emancipation, and each child's birth.

A deferred promise of freedom could thus coexist with continued slavery for the mother's descendants. The case cannot establish that Lytha's seven pregnancies were deliberately compelled. It does show the legal relation by which the children were separated from their mother's resulting political condition despite kinship. The court did not treat her status on the date of litigation as retroactively altering their status at birth.

A fifth Kentucky decision, *Banks' Administrator v. Marksberry* (13 Ky. 275, 1823), reportedly involved conveyance of the future increase of a woman called **Pen** independently of her current services. Alphonso (2023), pp. 31–32, interprets the case as recognition of an assignable right to future issue and quotes its comparison to future products of other property. The Delaware *Smith* opinion cites that earlier decision. **The 1823 text itself remains unrecovered.** Its substantive rule is therefore a hypothesis grounded in attributed secondary analysis until the full original report and deed are checked. That source-status boundary is an actual scientific result of the retrieval, not an inconvenience to hide.

## Finding IV. Jefferson valued future children as capital while measuring current labor as expenditure

Two letters supply evidence of what a particular Virginia slaveholder believed the ownership system should accomplish. In a January 17, 1819 letter to overseer **Joel Yancey**, Thomas Jefferson described **five reported infant deaths over four years**, urged that enslaved mothers have time to care for their infants, and valued surviving descendants over near-term field labor. The five deaths are an assertion in Jefferson's letter; independent registers and causes were not recovered. His expressed concern must not be equated with a rejection of their ownership or with a claim that enslaved families received adequate support.

In his **June 30, 1820** letter to **John Wayles Eppes**, Jefferson proposed transferring **20 enslaved people of varied ages and sexes** instead of **10 men** during a discussion of estate debt and family provision. His explanation explicitly treated women's prospective childbearing as a source of capital addition, unlike men's labor which was consumed by operations. This is a direct first-person admission of how he valued potential offspring in estate allocation. It is *not* evidence that a sale occurred, that any woman conceived by his direction, or that he knew the true number of babies a woman would bear.

A slaveholder's incentive to preserve captive infants is different from the capacity of their mothers to exercise free kinship. In this archive, infant survival mattered to the property owner because it promised a human asset. A family surviving or finding affection under that regime cannot be reduced to the owner's calculation. The [Sam and Louisa Everett testimony from 1936](../../../../bibliographies/everett-randolph1936.md) illustrates documented directed pairing as a separate mechanism from speculative rights over future descendants and from an owner's preference for infant survival. It has its own record, witness, and non-prevalence limitations.

## Four juridical operations, not a single factory

The case evidence compels a typed distinction between four operations: **(1) categorizing the child's slave/free condition at birth; (2) allocating the title to an already born person; (3) assigning present title or financial expectations in issue not yet born; and (4) changing status through an act of manumission.** The first may historically depend on a maternal-status rule; the second depends on the terms of ownership and inheritance; the third can exist without an actual future child; and the fourth can separate the fate of parent and child.

These relations should not be compressed into a single count. A person sold in the domestic slave trade is not thereby newly born; an owner writing “future issue” into a deed has not thereby produced a pregnancy; a newborn legally held enslaved was not necessarily conceived under explicit orders; and a child freed through a petition does not imply the enslaver who once claimed ownership intended to relinquish control. Each mechanism has different evidence and a different number that could responsibly be counted.

The original client hypothesis contains another distinction: a settler political-economic order can continue materially even where the nominally privileged population is not reproducing in the way its ideology promises. The present case study establishes how Black captive reproductive futures could become inputs to white enslaver property and intergenerational estate transfer. It cannot quantify how many white births that displaced or test whether an ideological search for two ideal white founders ever consumed the actually existing white population. That would require historical matched household fertility, natural increase, migration, class-specific family support and policy deliberations—not just the vocabulary of “increase.”

## Source bias, alternative interpretations, and next research

The court cases are selected because they survive and illustrate disputes. Four appellate opinions are not a probability sample of thousands of estates and cannot estimate prevalence of any rule. Enslaved mothers and children mostly appear as objects of litigation. Stephen's freedom petition is an unusually direct adverse claim within the court record, but still does not supply his full life history. A true case series must include original trial dockets, antebellum wills, freedom petitions, enslaved people's narratives and estate inventories, with an explicit inclusion rule and geographical frame.

A doctrinal historian could argue these opinions were principally ordinary will-construction exercises. At the level of procedural holding, this is true; but ordinary property rules governing future children reveal the material institutionalization of claims on lives that did not yet exist. A demographic historian could argue that enslaved people's own fertility and family relationships produced most births without owner's deliberate intervention. This paper neither denies nor quantifies that possibility. These explanations are compatible with legal expropriation; they undermine any attempted inference that the courts alone prove population-wide directed breeding.

The small case corpus shows at least three incompatible title rules, but which variations result from jurisdiction, time, drafting, emancipation terms or procedural history has not been statistically identified. Future research must recover the full **1823 Banks** opinion, collate **printed law reporters against the electronic transcriptions** (the Kentucky text contains OCR noise), find original wills and probate files, and use informed Black historical scholarship and survivor family records to recover agency outside the property record. For the client's broader argument, this is a necessary legal-economic ingredient, not a substitute for empirical cohort and political-economy tests.

## Conclusion

A court's treatment of future increase reveals a specific problem of settler social reproduction: birth could produce a human being whose anticipated life was already partitioned among other people's property claims. Those claims were nevertheless variable and disputed. The North Carolina decisions attached actual children to testamentary title; Delaware distinguished status from ownership and granted one petitioner freedom; Kentucky separated a mother's later manumission from her children's ongoing captivity. Jefferson, from the perspective of an enslaver, stated that future captive children added to his capital.

The most defensible conclusion is not that all birth into captivity was directed industrial breeding. It is that **the legal reproduction of an enslaving property's value could be organized around captive descendants while the descendants' own families and rights remained subordinated**. Such a regime could reproduce privileged settler property through the reproductive capacity of people excluded from its privileged political and kinship status.
