# Introduction: four histories under one name {#sec:introduction}

Settler institutions in the United States have often described their own continuation as the increase of a white population. This study argues that the description joins four histories that can move in different directions, and that separating them shows which of them those institutions actually preserved when the histories conflicted.

## The four histories

The phrase *white increase* can refer to any of four objects. Each has its own evidence and its own way of failing.

1. **Numerical reproduction.** Births, deaths and survival among people a given source counts as white. Its evidence is demographic: registration, census and reconstructed series.
2. **Classification.** The rules that decide who counts as white. Its evidence is legal and administrative: statutes, court decisions, census instructions and the records of the officials who applied them. A classified population can grow or shrink because the rules change, without any change in births.
3. **Eligibility to found the preferred future.** The narrower standard that names some people as proper progenitors and others as unfit. Its evidence is ideological and administrative: pronatalist and eugenic writing, sterilization boards and marriage law.
4. **Institutional reproduction.** The continuation of property, jurisdiction, labor relations and authority. Its evidence is material: land title, inheritance, labor contracts and the reproduction of slaveholding capital.

These four histories are often treated as one history. When they are, a reader cannot ask the question this study is built around: **when the four conflicted, which did settler institutions preserve, and which did they treat as expendable?**

## The originating hypothesis and its evidence status

The research began from a strong hypothesis, posed as a research direction rather than as a finding. In that hypothesis, a regime that calls itself white may spend the lives and reproductive chances of the people it currently classifies as white in pursuit of an ideal future population those people never embody. In the limiting case, the search exhausts the actual population before it reaches its own success condition.

This study does not demonstrate that limiting case. It treats the hypothesis as a conditional claim and asks what evidence would be needed to test it (Part I, section 9). The study states every claim with one of three evidence statuses:

- **Observed.** The cited sources directly show it.
- **Mechanistically plausible.** It is consistent with the sources and with a stated mechanism, but rival explanations have not been excluded.
- **Not established.** The available evidence does not decide it, and the paper says what evidence would.

## Design: a revealed-priority comparison

The study does not treat *whiteness* as an actor with intentions. It compares specific decisions by named institutions in situations where the four histories pulled apart. A legislature that relaxes a purity rule to protect an elite family has revealed that it values that family's status over the rule. A court that divides a child's status from the title to that child has revealed how the law organized reproduction as property. The comparison asks what each decision preserved. It does not ask what a racial category wanted.

Four rival explanations remain open throughout, and each part tests the ones it can:

- routine property accumulation without any reproductive ideal;
- changing classification that alters a count without altering births;
- preservation of elite status within the classified population; and
- independent policies that happen to coexist without coordination.

## The four parts

**Part I, *The Future White*,** compares Benjamin Franklin's 1751 population arithmetic, early twentieth-century race-suicide and eugenic pronatalism, and Virginia's racial-integrity legislation of 1924–1930. It finds that numerical increase and purity were neither identical nor uniformly prioritized. Virginia's ancestry exception, defended and then proposed for widening to protect favored genealogies, shows a purity standard that bent when strict application threatened influential people. The proposed 1926 House broadening did not become law. The enacted 1930 law, and the Pamunkey opposition to it, are examined separately.

**Part II, *Birth into Captivity*,** reconstructs the share of births in the historical U.S. statistical population that were births into hereditary slavery. From modeled captive births (Hacker 2020) and reconstructed national births (Haines and Sutch), it finds 5,167,522 of 30,038,000 births, or **17.20%**, in 1800–1849, and 16.82% in 1790–1859 with an assumed 1790s denominator. These are not estimates of directed conception, and they are not shares of all births on Indigenous lands.

**Part III, *Whose Future Increase?*,** reads seven primary legal documents, which yield 21 observations, from North Carolina, Delaware, Kentucky and Virginia. It separates four juridical operations: the child's status at birth, title to an already born child, claims on issue not yet conceived, and later manumission. The cases show reproduction organized as property, and they also show disputed and variable outcomes, including a successful freedom claim.

**Part IV, *Transatlantic Population Circuits*,** examines how European institutions sent people, for example through British convict transportation, redemptioner debt and the 1880 Gersau emigration subsidy. It examines how American institutions received them through labor, land and citizenship rules, and how American models returned to Europe. Nineteenth-century U.S. growth came mostly from natural increase. Net migration accounted for 11.9% of national growth in 1790–1800 and 32.3% in 1850–1860. Those shares do not measure white-specific replacement.

## What the parts can and cannot establish together

The parts use different kinds of evidence: documentary-conceptual, quantitative, legal-case and archival-comparative. Their findings converge on one institutional pattern, but this study does not combine them into a single causal law. A shared word such as *increase* does not license that inference. The Conclusion states what the convergence shows, and it lists the cohort, household and policy-linked evidence that the stronger hypothesis would require.

## Sources, reproducibility and disclosure

Each quantitative claim in this paper is stated once more as data in `claims/claims.json` and recomputed from the archived source records. The `make check` command fails if any stated number disagrees with its derivation. Each cited source carries a reading grade recording how much of it was read: full primary text, publisher abstract, edited transcription, or secondary report. Appendix A maps each claim to its sources and grades. The study's vocabulary is published as an OWL ontology and SKOS concept scheme, and its build provenance as a W3C PROV-O graph.

AI systems performed most of the source retrieval, drafting and quantitative reconstruction under the direction of the accountable author. This work is disclosed on the title page and in `docs/ai-disclosure.md`. Readers should weigh each claim by its evidence status and reading grade, and the paper is built to make that weighing possible.
