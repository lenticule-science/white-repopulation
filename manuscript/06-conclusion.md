# Conclusion: what settler institutions preserved {#sec:conclusion}

Across four kinds of evidence, the institutions examined here preserved the authority to classify people and to appropriate reproduction more reliably than they preserved the reproductive opportunities of every person they classified as white.

## The convergent finding

Each part reaches this result through its own evidence, and each part's result can stand without the others.

- **Classification bent to protect status** (Part I, observed). Virginia's purity rule carried an ancestry exception for favored genealogies. The legislature considered widening that exception in 1926, and in 1930 it enacted distinct rules. The standard that claimed to protect a race changed so that particular families remained inside it.
- **Reproduction was organized as property without membership** (Part II, observed; Part III, observed). About one U.S. birth in six in 1800–1849 was a birth into hereditary slavery. Antebellum property law could assign title to children, and even to issue not yet conceived, while those children's families and rights stayed subordinate. Settler property grew through the reproduction of people the settler polity excluded.
- **Eligibility narrowed within the classified population** (Part I, observed from secondary scholarship and administrative records). Race-suicide pronatalism urged births among people judged fit, and eugenic policy discouraged or prevented births among people judged unfit. Some of the people judged unfit were themselves classified white.
- **Settler growth drew on institutions that selected migrants** (Part IV, observed for named cases). Sending authorities financed, compelled or conditioned departure. Receiving authorities admitted, employed and settled people under eligibility rules, and their models were later studied and borrowed in Europe.

Taken together, these findings support a model of **conditional selective futurity**. In this model, the future described as worthy of reproduction can be redefined repeatedly, while existing people are classified, excluded or deprived in its name. Meanwhile property, jurisdiction and authority continue through other people's reproduction. The status of this model is *mechanistically plausible*. It fits every part's evidence, and two rival explanations remain open: routine accumulation that needed no reproductive ideal, and independent policies that coexisted without coordination.

## What is not established

The stronger proposition remains **not established**. In that proposition, a self-reinforcing search for an ideal founding population exhausts the actual white-classified population before the ideal is reached. The evidence assembled here also contains counterevidence:

- The white-classified census population of North Carolina grew from 1950 to 1960, during a period of state sterilization.
- Nineteenth-century national growth came mostly from natural increase.
- Purity standards were relaxed as well as tightened.

A test of the proposition would need four things:

1. explicit historical eligibility standards and their revisions;
2. feasible policy alternatives that decision-makers rejected;
3. cohort-level fertility, survival and migration for defined populations, with reclassification recorded separately from birth and death; and
4. evidence of shared finance, authority or decision-making. Without that evidence, policies that coexist cannot be treated as one selecting system.

## The question this changes

The finding moves the research question. The question is no longer who will be the last descendants counted as white. It is who holds the authority to defer the promised future, who is made to bear the costs of that deferral, and which institutions reproduce themselves without delivering what their reproductive ideology promises.

## Next research

The parts name their own outstanding work. The work that would most change this paper's conclusions is:

- recovering the original 1926 Virginia House and Senate journals and the 1930 implementation record;
- independently re-extracting both demographic source series and quantifying their error beyond the 1790s sensitivity range;
- collating printed judicial reports, wills and freedom petitions against the electronic transcriptions used here;
- tracing the 29 Gersau emigrants of 1880 through passenger, census and household records; and
- obtaining independent assessment by historians and demographers, including against the recent work of Nicholls and Younis, which this paper treats as prior results rather than as its own contribution.

Each of these is open work. None of them is reported here as done.
