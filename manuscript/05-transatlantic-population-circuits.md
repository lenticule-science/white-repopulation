# Exporting Subjects, Importing Settlers, Returning Models

## Transatlantic population circuits and American settler reproduction, 1718–1935

**Status and custody (17 October 2026).** Original comparative *research draft* in Lenticule Science / Sociology Science; not accepted, peer-reviewed, or submitted. A private client's earlier research question and later extension generated this inquiry, followed by an additional interpretive suggestion from the 561 Group. Their private wording and identifying information are deliberately omitted. Client authorship, approvals, interpretation, and quotation rights remain open. The intent is to investigate the complete research question, not to presume its answer. Historical eugenic classifications are treated as constructions of power, not biological truths.

### Abstract

Did US settler institutions reproduce themselves less through a self-sustaining white-born population than through a transatlantic circuit that moved precarious European people into American labor, land, kinship, and citizenship regimes, then circulated American practices back to Europe? This study disaggregates three processes too often collapsed as "white increase": population accounting (births, deaths, migration, and classification), institutional reproduction (land title, labor contracts, coerced childbirth, jurisdiction, inheritance, and privileged citizenship), and outward circulation of political/social models. Historical demographic estimates contradict the strongest national version of migrant replacement: the United States' nineteenth-century growth was mainly natural increase. Yet primary records demonstrate purposive state and municipal engineering of movement. The British transportation system (1718–1775), the 1834 Poor Law's assisted-emigration provision, municipal records from Gersau, Switzerland (1880–1881), the US Homestead Act (1862), and the federal Immigration Act (1864) reveal different, nonidentical arrangements for relocating people and converting them into colonial labor or settler property holders. Racialized eligibility selected which newcomers were incorporated into a favored reproductive future. Two demonstrable forms of outward circulation are French commissioned study of American penal institutions in 1831 and German attention to US racial legal precedents in the 1930s. The proposed contribution is a **dual-screened population-and-model circuit**: sending jurisdictions and receiving jurisdictions classified people differently, and institutional reproduction may persist even while racial demographic categories change. The paper states specific rival explanations and archival tests, without equating immigrant indenture with hereditary racial slavery, nationality with statelessness, or a cultural analogy with proven bilateral causation.

## 1. The commissioned proposition and what could falsify it

The opening problem concerns **material social reproduction**. A settler population is not reproduced solely by biological fertility: households require access to food, land, wages, housing, care, safety, kin networks, and political membership. A settler jurisdiction may maintain its territorial and political arrangements via new arrivals even if particular neighborhoods, labor forces, or settler lineages fail to replace themselves locally. Its membership category can expand or contract by legislation or social recognition.

The stronger original conjecture is testable: some settler societies, locales, or recognized lineages may depend on immigration for their persistence, perhaps because powerful European institutions generated economically precarious outmigrants whose relocation was useful to settler development. The further conjecture is **recursive international feedback**: after importing people, American institutions produced forms of culture, industrial management, penal organization, and racial legislation observed or appropriated in Europe. This is a proposed causal **circuit**, not a presumption of a single European or US policymaker's integrated design.

The following claims must not be presumed:
1. That the *national* US population in the nineteenth century lacked natural increase.
2. That all European migrants were legally stateless, enslaved, deported, or subsidized.
3. That a single "feudal Europe" enacted a homogeneous bilateral settlement pact with an independent United States across colonial and modern periods.
4. That outward influence proves that America *invented* the influenced practice or that Europe accepted it uncritically.
5. That net immigration into the total population measures net *white* settler reproduction, or that those counted as immigrants all became landholding settlers.
6. That the presence of eugenic ideology proves its demographic effects, or that a discriminatory source's allegations about migrants are true.

## 2. Units of analysis: distinguish six reproductions

**Biological/population flow:** Define a jurisdiction g, period t, and historically documented classification rule c. Then the stock's change is births minus deaths plus entry minus exit plus changes due to territorial boundaries and classification. A racially classified subgroup additionally requires people entering or leaving that *classification* without moving or being born/dying. Children of migrants count as births where born, not as contemporaneous immigration, while descent must be analyzed by parental origin in a separate cohort dataset.

**Household continuity:** Formation, dissolution, marriage permission, wages, shelter, care work and heirs. Survival of a household is not identical to increase of aggregate heads.

**Labor force continuity:** Employers may replace labor via immigration, internal migration, wage coercion, contract debt, temporary employment, or captive birth. Do not assume that each laborer is or becomes a settler.

**Territorial/jurisdictional continuity:** Land claims, Indigenous dispossession, survey, title, treaties, military enforcement and jurisdiction. This dimension cannot be deduced from national population arithmetic.

**Eligibility / the "future" population:** Migration restrictions, naturalization, marriage laws, racial classification, eugenic exclusions and pronatalist preferences set differential membership conditions. The classification "white" is itself historically political and uneven; it is not a stable biological input variable.

**Return flows:** Remittances, return migration, commercial models, reports, letters, law, institutions, administrative techniques and cultural works transmitted toward Europe; not all return flows are "culture" and not every comparison is direct borrowing.

The paper complements—not displaces—the existing studies on [selective futurity](2026-10-17-manuscript-selective-futurity-and-settler-continuity.md), [births into captivity](2026-10-17-manuscript-captive-births-and-settler-reproduction.md), and [reproductive futures as property](2026-10-17-manuscript-reproductive-futures-as-property.md). These address independent dimensions and should not be forced into one causation estimate.

## 3. First result: national numerical replacement and institutional reproduction diverge

Michael R. Haines's 1994 historical reconstruction estimates that the US population grew predominantly through natural increase between 1790 and 1920. The historical growth-accounting series reproduced by the National Academies attributes **11.9% of US population increase in 1790–1800**, **32.3% in 1850–1860**, and **35.3% in 1900–1910** to net international migration. The respective remainder came overwhelmingly from natural increase under this accounting. These are **all-population national period estimates**, not directly observed white-specific or local settler lineage survival figures.

The reverse generalization is also unsafe. Statistics Canada estimated that **97.6% of Canada's 2023 national growth** came from international migration (permanent plus non-permanent) and **2.4%** from natural increase. Australia's Bureau of Statistics reported for the year ending December 2024 net overseas migration of **340,800** and natural increase of **105,200**, of approximately **445,900** total growth (rounded), or ~76.4% attributed to net overseas migration. This establishes that immigration-dominated national growth is possible in named later settler states/periods, **not** that these immigrants were white or all settlers or that nineteenth-century US growth had the same structure. In particular temporary residents must not be silently treated as permanent settler reproduction.

The claim to test thus becomes: **where, when, among whom, and in what institutional function** was the reproduction of settler rule dependent upon migrants even when national growth was mostly births? Counties, urban immigrant neighborhoods, railway construction, newly opened homesteading tracts, white-eligible citizenship groups and colonial labor systems may have different answers.

## 4. Sending people: four materially different mechanisms

### 4.1 British convict transportation (1718–1775): a colonial regime, not a US treaty

Britain's 1718 legal and Treasury-backed convict transportation regime exported people convicted by its courts to **British American colonies**. A. Roger Ekirch estimates roughly **50,000** transported convicts, 1718–1775; the US Department of Labor's labor-history account describes statutory seven- and fourteen-year service terms. Ekirch documents a Treasury contract in 1718 at **£3 per person** with a merchant; private traders purchased and marketed bound labor in the colonies. The transported people were compelled by British penal power, and American colonial masters exploited the resulting labor. They were not sent as part of a government-to-government deal with the United States, which did not exist as an independent republic in 1718. A term of convict service is not juridically identical to hereditary racial chattel slavery.

Sources: Ekirch, *Bound for America* (1990), https://academic.oup.com/book/32978 ; merchant contract abstract, https://academic.oup.com/book/32978/chapter-abstract/279117768 ; US Department of Labor, https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/chapter1 .

### 4.2 Redemptioners: transport debt transferred into American employment

Mid-eighteenth-century German migrant Gottlieb Mittelberger described a market at Philadelphia in which people unable to pay passage could not disembark until purchasers undertook to cover travel debts in exchange for service. His testimony is reproduced with editorial provenance by George Mason University's *History Matters* project. This mechanism was **merchant- and migrant-contract mediated**, not automatically a European sovereign's deportation order. It could expose migrants to harsh constraint, selection, and unequal bargaining positions. Farley Grubb's peer-reviewed quantitative study of **4,455 German and British servant contracts, 1771–1804**, finds mean servant compensation comparable with resident free-labor compensation in the sampled auctions, while recognizing restrictions and variance. That evidence is an important competing interpretation of the market, not proof that all conditions were benign or that Mittelberger fabricated his experience. Analyze legal and practical agency separately.

Sources: Mittelberger, *History Matters*, https://historymatters.gmu.edu/d/5712/ ; Grubb, *Journal of Economic History*, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/auction-of-redemptioner-servants-philadelphia-17711804-an-economic-analysis/84BCB0E6EE449B152FC263DBDA384526 .

### 4.3 Parish, workhouse and landlord assistance: collective administration, not universal shipping

The English **Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834** allowed home parishes to fund emigration for poor residents. Britain's National Archives identifies Poor Law Commission records, Poor Law Union files **MH 12**, correspondence **MH 13/252** (1853–54), and **MH 19/22** (1836–76) for direct reconstruction. Irish workhouses and landlords also organized or subsidized passage during the Great Famine; Hidetaka Hirota studies the changing population origins and their relation to US anti-Irish restriction.

Scale limits are indispensable. An *English Historical Review* assessment of Gerard Moran estimates that roughly **100,000 famine-era emigrants received even minor landlord subsidies, less than 3% of 1835–1855 outflow**. The estimate of up to **300,000** assisted Irish departures to North America over the nineteenth century was dwarfed by approximately eight million total departures. Assistance happened, but the stronger thesis that most arrivals were institutionally shipped at official expense is contradicted for this population and interval. Gary Howells's local study of three English counties also warns against simplifying all assistance as expelling people the sponsors considered worthless; some programs were articulated as reciprocal welfare and paternalistic aid.

Sources: National Archives, https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/emigration/ ; Hirota (2017), https://academic.oup.com/book/9224/chapter-abstract/155896081 ; EHR review (2006), https://academic.oup.com/ehr/article-abstract/CXXI/490/329/458602 ; Howells (2003), https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1468-229X.00281 .

### 4.4 Gersau, Switzerland (1880): a direct, unusually high-resolution sending decision

A municipal assembly of **Gersau** on **22 August 1880** adopted an emigration-subsidy program during unemployment. Its decision, municipal explanations, and US consular correspondence survive together in the US State Department's published *Foreign Relations* documents dated **24 January 1881**. This is a concrete research-grade case, unlike diffuse claims that "Europe" exported unwanted bodies. Directly recorded rules:

- A Gersau town council assessed eligibility; applicants had to be judged physically and mentally sound, have a destination, and leave dependents supported.
- Adult members could receive **250 francs**; sons aged 10–18 **100 francs**, with separate lower rates for younger boys.
- An applicant receiving aid relinquished the **benefits of communal membership for twenty years**, with provisions to repay the subsidy (and in some cases interest) if returning earlier and seeking restoration of those benefits.
- The council made individual contracts and used agents for Lucerne–New York passages.
- Gersau reported **29 male emigrants and 5,755 francs of assistance** for the year 1880.
- The council said it could end the program if local employment recovered.

This is an instance of **selective externally financed relocation combined with a reduction of future municipal obligations**, with explicit transport contracting. It does **not** mean these people became legally stateless; the broader 1855 Swiss Federal Council position reproduced in the diplomatic compilation expressly contemplated continuing Swiss nationality despite acquisition of another citizenship. Nor is the pre-1883 rule in itself evidence of a modern genetics/eugenics program: its fitness filter is documented, its intellectual lineage is not.

The US consular and diplomatic correspondents regarded some subsidized migration as undesirable and protested particular cases. A related July 1881 exchange confirms US diplomatic opposition to a Swiss commune assisting a named penitentiary inmate to come to America. Therefore this evidence points to **strategic interaction and conflict among municipal, federal, private, and receiving actors**, not a proved agreement to jointly empty European poorhouses.

The 1881 diplomatic review quotes a **19 March 1855 Swiss Federal Council letter to US Minister Fay**, explaining municipal membership-based compensation and requesting favorable treatment of Swiss emigrants. It is evidence of **government-to-government communication**, but the 1855 original has **not** yet been independently collated, and the 1881 US commentator's description of US concessions is a claim needing verification in receiving-side policies. The same diplomat analogized compelled movement to old European feudal practices; that *analogy in a political complaint* cannot establish that 1880 Switzerland was a feudal state.

Primary loci: resolution and enclosures, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1881/d676 ; extended 1880 diplomatic analysis with 1855 Federal Council excerpt, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1881/d671 ; July 1881 federal intervention, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1881/d703 .

**Proposed original archival investigation:** trace the 29 named recipients in Gersau municipal minutes, passports/emigration contracts, transport-agent invoices, Castle Garden entry lists, US census occupations and households, marriages, deaths and any return petitions. Cross-check the original German resolution against the US translation. The current American diplomatic source reports a municipal document but cannot establish individual life-course outcomes. Verify the 1855 Swiss exchange from the original legation record and identify the US policy that supposedly granted special treatment. This single local study can test whether origin-side relief obligations were *actually* transferred to American households or institutions.

## 5. Receiving people: selective labor, land, citizenship and territory

The **Homestead Act of 20 May 1862** offered specified adult citizens and those formally declaring intent to become citizens entry into federally claimed land, ordinarily 160 acres, conditioned on residence, improvements, and later title. The National Park Service explicitly documents the law's intended encouragement of European immigration. Its resource was **land**: an institutional mechanism to turn eligible migrants and other entrants into landholding settlers. The lands were not socially empty: Indigenous nations maintained political claims, families, and relations to them. The Park Service documents links between homesteading and Indigenous displacement; its summary traces reservation acreage from 138 million in 1887 to 48 million in 1934 in the context of allotment. Manu Karuka situates railway finance and Chinese labor together with Lakota, Cheyenne, and Pawnee jurisdiction and dispossession. Here one cannot use a white immigration account while omitting Indigenous territory and non-European labor.

The **Act to Encourage Immigration, 4 July 1864** established federal immigration administration, regulated foreign-made labor agreements, and explicitly permitted qualifying arrivals to pledge **up to twelve months' wages** against transport costs. The enacted text also expressly disclaimed authorization to create slavery or servitude. Thus the statute documents purposeful recruitment and monetization of migrant labor capacity but directly rebuts the *literal legal inference* that all such migrants were enslaved. Labor exploitation, debt dependency, or coercion must be demonstrated through contracts, enforcement and wages, rather than asserted from the existence of passage finance.

The **1790 naturalization rule**, largely limiting naturalization to free white persons, and the **1870 extension** to persons of African nativity/descent show that legal recognition as a future citizen was selectively produced. **Chinese Exclusion (1882)** demonstrates that some non-European workers whose labor could be used in an expanding economy were denied a comparable avenue of admission and citizenship. The **Immigration Act of 1924** curtailed southern/eastern European arrivals, privileged particular earlier national-origin populations, and excluded Asian immigrants under its rules. These are historically separate policies, not a single consistent racial algorithm.

At the same time, the legal and social boundary of whiteness requires careful treatment. Noel Ignatiev's history of Irish incorporation is influential, but Cybelle Fox and Thomas Guglielmo (2012) challenge overly simple claims that previously legally nonwhite Europeans merely "became white." We must separately trace legal racial eligibility, social acceptance, privilege, local discrimination, and racial violence. The relevant working object is **membership decisions under dated institutions**, not an ahistorical count of a fixed white race.

## 6. Eugenics as the attempted control of imported generations

Eugenics is **not** an optional supplementary chapter to this inquiry. It makes explicit that people were understood by certain institutions not only as **current workers and residents** but as **potential ancestors of future citizens**. "Selective futurity" describes the conflict among current useful labor, preferred future ancestry, and the continued institutional authority to classify both.

Harry H. Laughlin's testimony to Congress and his Eugenics Record Office networks helped press biological claims against newer immigrants. The **1924 Johnson–Reed** quota framework used 1890-based national-origin distributions to favor selected national stocks. An immediately contemporary critical response exists: Joseph M. Gillman's **1924** *American Journal of Sociology* article argued Laughlin's datasets were incomplete and his statistical conclusion unfounded. The eugenic inference was disputed at the time; do not reproduce its population judgments as true. Heidi Nicholls (2026) demonstrates that the settler aims of early sociological policy could be advanced by apparently opposite eugenic strategies: uncompromising purity *or* assimilation/amalgamation. Thus immigrant inclusion and immigrant exclusion could both be mobilized in defense of settler jurisdiction.

A theoretically sharper question follows from joining sending-side Gersau to US immigration law: **Was the institution trying to maximize actual settlers, maximize the population recognized as fit *future ancestors*, maximize extractable present labor, minimize receiving/public-relief costs, or secure territorial/legal continuity?** Those aims differ and their relative priority can only be inferred through choices when they conflict.

The reproduction of enslaved African-descended people was governed through a materially distinct legal regime, with hereditary slave status, sexual violence, kinship deprivation, and property claims to children. Jennifer L. Morgan's work documents that foundation. The separate Lenticule demographic manuscript reconstructs approximately **17.20%** of recorded/modelled US births during 1800–1849 as births into legal captivity, conditional on specified sources and limitations. This work must not be merged with a story in which all European migrants were slaves: such conflation would erase specific Black reproductive expropriation and its intergenerational legality.

## 7. Returning social and governmental models to Europe: three tests

### 7.1 Tocqueville and Beaumont, 1831–1833: observation requested from Europe

Alexis de Tocqueville and Gustave de Beaumont entered the US in **1831** under a mission sanctioned by the French government to study penitentiaries, observed broader American political and social arrangements, and returned with material that informed an **1833 published comparative study** and Tocqueville's later *Democracy in America*. The book was **not an official government report** despite their government-sponsored mission, as the Cambridge historical treatment emphasizes. Yale University Press's edition of Tocqueville's letters identifies the governmental prison-study commission and the letters returned to France. Here Europeans explicitly sought **American institutional experience as potential policy knowledge**; the case is more precise than the metaphor of America sending culture. It shows an **outbound observation and translation** channel, not an American state program designed to export lessons.

Sources: Yale UP, https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300153835/letters-from-america/ ; Cambridge, https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/anthem-companion-to-alexis-de-tocqueville/tocqueville-and-beaumont-on-the-us-penitentiary-system/9CEA78F5482B58253511EEDDB065D454 ; BnF 1831 mission notice, https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb31476137q .

### 7.2 Fordism: US industrial labor organization debated as a European model

Antonio Gramsci's *Americanism and Fordism* analyzes attempts to import US industrial organization and the social disciplining of labor into an older European social structure. This is relevant because the export was not merely an artistic object: labor rhythms, managerial disciplines, household assumptions and mass consumer culture can operate as **social-reproductive techniques**. A historical reading must distinguish *discussion of a model* from established adoption of an identical institution. Contemporary scholarship on Gramsci's treatment of industrial and reproductive discipline documents the distinction.

Sources: Gramsci, *Selections from the Prison Notebooks*, https://files.libcom.org/files/Gramsci%20-%20Selections%20from%20the%20Prison%20Notebooks.pdf ; Redini (2023), https://journals.uniurb.it/index.php/igj/article/view/4060 ; Torigian (1999), https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/abs/occupation-of-the-factories-paris-1936-flint-1937/7E85B0855E6958614B63D658B785C566 .

### 7.3 Germany and the US, 1920s–1930s: policy feedback via racial law and eugenic networks

A more direct record of backward legal influence is James Q. Whitman's *Hitler's American Model* (2017): Nazi legal planners took sustained interest in American restrictive citizenship and anti-miscegenation rules during the construction of their own **1935 Nuremberg Laws**. Stefan Kühl's *The Nazi Connection* (1994) documents transnational contacts among eugenicists and influence in both directions, rather than a simplistic American sole origin. Some American eugenicists also endorsed German policies. This is a **documented appropriation of American legal precedent** with identifiable channels, yet German political movements had their own antecedents and agendas. It is not evidence that migration from a specified village like Gersau caused those legal exports generations later.

Sources: Whitman (2017), https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/professor-james-whitman-publishes-hitlers-american-model ; Kühl (1994), https://academic.oup.com/book/48064 ; a scholarly review with caveats, https://academic.oup.com/jah/article-abstract/105/3/737/5247908 .

## 8. Provisional model: a dual-screened transatlantic circuit

A parsimonious proposed sequence is:

**European household insecurity or penal judgment** → **origin-side rule for assisted/free/coerced departure** → **merchant, state or family financing and Atlantic passage** → **receiving-side entry and eligibility determination** → **labor assignment and/or land-and-household reproduction** → **citizenship, inheritance and population classification** → **texts, administration, culture and policy models returned through transatlantic networks**.

There need not be a single institutional operator spanning the entire chain. "Circuit" is appropriate if **identified transmissions and feedback** can be demonstrated for specific actors and time periods; it is otherwise a heuristic arrangement of separate events. The hypothesized **dual screen** comprises the origin's departure eligibility and the destination's entry/citizenship eligibility. The same person can be desirable for the origin to lose yet undesirable for the receiving state to admit. Gersau's selection rules and the American consul's protests show this tension at the level of documented institutions.

**Distinct outcome measures:**
- \`D_nat(g,t)\`: share of jurisdictional population growth attributable to net migration, with births and deaths independently reported. Does *not* measure racial membership.
- \`D_lineage(g,c,t)\`: cohort survival and descendant stocks for specified historical membership categories, with conversions and reclassifications separately recorded.
- \`D_local(g,t)\`: fraction of new households or resident labor sourced through geographic inward migration, including *internal* US migration separately.
- \`D_property(g,t)\`: observed changes in land title and control tied to migration, including Indigenous jurisdictional origin and source of survey/title.
- \`D_cost(o,t)\`: sending locality relief expenses, travel subsidies and subsequent relief/pension claims before and after a funded departure, net of repayments.
- \`D_exploitation(g,t)\`: actual wages, mobility restrictions, debt contracts, violence, legal status and exit rights. Never infer this from migrant identity alone.
- \`D_return(e,t)\`: traceable citation, translation, personnel, legal borrowing, procurement, or organizational adoption from a specified American practice to a specified European receiver.

**Comparative hypotheses and falsifiers:**
- **H1, replacement at scale:** Migration exceeded natural increase for the *specified* settler population, region or period. Falsified in the Haines national all-population accounting for nineteenth-century US periods; open for bounded subgroups and some modern countries.
- **H2, official population export:** Sending bodies financially supported emigration because continuing local obligations exceeded relocation costs. Gersau provides plausibility via subsidy and twenty-year communal benefit surrender. Causal proof requires actual fiscal accounts, other expenditures, and testimonies; rival: genuine assistance to voluntary movers.
- **H3, immigrant conversion:** Receiving institutions channeled migrants into landholding, employed, and legally favored settler roles. Requires linked household, land patent, employment and status records, not a Homestead statute alone; rivals: migrants entered cities, returned home, or were excluded.
- **H4, selection over numbers:** Restrictive gatekeepers accepted economic loss or foregone population increase to maintain an ideal future group. Needs legislation, legislative trade-offs, and demographic cohorts; rival: labor/welfare, diplomatic or wartime reasons produced selection.
- **H5, outbound model:** American practices measurably changed a named European institution. Tocqueville proves observation/reporting; Whitman documents influential legal borrowing; a generalized "Europe received American culture" remains unmeasured without specific transmissions.
- **H6, reproduced sovereignty rather than lineage:** Settler territorial claims or economic institutions persisted despite turnover or reclassification of nominal beneficiaries. Needs administrative continuity, beneficiary turnover and Indigenous-source jurisdiction data; rival: existing households themselves reproduced most relevant relations locally.

**Important negative evidence:** British government transport before US independence cannot show US diplomatic cooperation; Irish official/landlord assistance represented a small minority of total migration; 1881 Swiss-US friction defeats a universal harmonious bilateral program; 1864 US law formally excluded slavery/servitude from its immigration-financing contract authority; historical national natural increase refutes the blanket claim of US demographic incapacity.

## 10. Conclusion

The literal conjecture that American national settlement survived chiefly because Europe continually supplied white replacements is too strong for the early US demographic evidence. But a different, demonstrably researched question survives: **how did institutions on each side of the Atlantic move people who were costly or constrained in one jurisdiction into potentially useful labor, property, and future-parent categories in another, while selecting who was permitted to belong—and how did the receiving society become a source of transferable political and social techniques?**

The contribution of this draft is a case-backed design rather than a rhetorical declaration. The archival record already supplies three distinct contrasts: British legally forced transportation, Swiss municipally financed and selectively eligible departure, and US state-assisted reception through labor/land rules. It also supplies two documented modes of outward observation and policy borrowing. No single historical period or collective racial "will" unifies them. A proper explanation must show which actors' decisions, in which year, changed which person's material ability to live, work, own land, maintain family relations, or belong to a polity—and which European institution demonstrably used the resulting knowledge. That is a researchable history of **settler social reproduction as institutional, demographic, and intellectual circulation**.
