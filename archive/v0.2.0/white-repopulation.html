Abstract
Settler institutions in the United States have described their future as the increase of a white population. That description joins four histories that move independently: the births and survival of people counted as white; the changing rules for who counts; eligibility to found a preferred future population; and the reproduction of property, jurisdiction and labor relations. This study separates them and asks which one institutions preserved when they conflicted. Four documentary and quantitative strands answer in turn. Franklin’s population arithmetic, race-suicide pronatalism and Virginia’s racial-integrity law show a purity ideal that bent when its rigid application threatened elite genealogies. A reconstruction of births, 1790–1859, finds that roughly one U.S. birth in six (17.20% in 1800–1849) was a birth into hereditary slavery, a reproduction of property that conferred no membership. Antebellum property law divided a child’s status, title and anticipated issue across time. Transatlantic sending, receiving and policy-borrowing cases show settler growth drawing on selected, indebted and assisted migrants through case-specific institutions rather than one coordinated design. Across the strands, institutions preserved the authority to classify and to appropriate reproduction more reliably than the reproductive opportunities of every person classified white. The stronger proposition, that a deferred ideal can exhaust the population it claims to protect, remains a conditional hypothesis; the paper states the cohort and institutional evidence that would test it.

Introduction: four histories under one name

Settler institutions in the United States have often described their own continuation as the increase of a white population. This study argues that the description joins four histories that can move in different directions, and that separating them shows which of them those institutions actually preserved when the histories conflicted.

The four histories

The phrase white increase can refer to any of four objects. Each has its own evidence and its own way of failing.

  1. Numerical reproduction. Births, deaths and survival among people a given source counts as white. Its evidence is demographic: registration, census and reconstructed series.
  2. Classification. The rules that decide who counts as white. Its evidence is legal and administrative: statutes, court decisions, census instructions and the records of the officials who applied them. A classified population can grow or shrink because the rules change, without any change in births.
  3. Eligibility to found the preferred future. The narrower standard that names some people as proper progenitors and others as unfit. Its evidence is ideological and administrative: pronatalist and eugenic writing, sterilization boards and marriage law.
  4. Institutional reproduction. The continuation of property, jurisdiction, labor relations and authority. Its evidence is material: land title, inheritance, labor contracts and the reproduction of slaveholding capital.

These four histories are often treated as one history. When they are, a reader cannot ask the question this study is built around: when the four conflicted, which did settler institutions preserve, and which did they treat as expendable?

The originating hypothesis and its evidence status

The research began from a strong hypothesis, posed as a research direction rather than as a finding. In that hypothesis, a regime that calls itself white may spend the lives and reproductive chances of the people it currently classifies as white in pursuit of an ideal future population those people never embody. In the limiting case, the search exhausts the actual population before it reaches its own success condition.

This study does not demonstrate that limiting case. It treats the hypothesis as a conditional claim and asks what evidence would be needed to test it (Part I, section 9). The study states every claim with one of three evidence statuses:

  • Observed. The cited sources directly show it.
  • Mechanistically plausible. It is consistent with the sources and with a stated mechanism, but rival explanations have not been excluded.
  • Not established. The available evidence does not decide it, and the paper says what evidence would.

Design: a revealed-priority comparison

The study does not treat whiteness as an actor with intentions. It compares specific decisions by named institutions in situations where the four histories pulled apart. A legislature that relaxes a purity rule to protect an elite family has revealed that it values that family’s status over the rule. A court that divides a child’s status from the title to that child has revealed how the law organized reproduction as property. The comparison asks what each decision preserved. It does not ask what a racial category wanted.

Four rival explanations remain open throughout, and each part tests the ones it can:

  • routine property accumulation without any reproductive ideal;
  • changing classification that alters a count without altering births;
  • preservation of elite status within the classified population; and
  • independent policies that happen to coexist without coordination.

The four parts

Part I, The Future White, compares Benjamin Franklin’s 1751 population arithmetic, early twentieth-century race-suicide and eugenic pronatalism, and Virginia’s racial-integrity legislation of 1924–1930. It finds that numerical increase and purity were neither identical nor uniformly prioritized. Virginia’s ancestry exception, defended and then proposed for widening to protect favored genealogies, shows a purity standard that bent when strict application threatened influential people. The proposed 1926 House broadening did not become law. The enacted 1930 law, and the Pamunkey opposition to it, are examined separately.

Part II, Birth into Captivity, reconstructs the share of births in the historical U.S. statistical population that were births into hereditary slavery. From modeled captive births (Hacker 2020) and reconstructed national births (Haines and Sutch), it finds 5,167,522 of 30,038,000 births, or 17.20%, in 1800–1849, and 16.82% in 1790–1859 with an assumed 1790s denominator. These are not estimates of directed conception, and they are not shares of all births on Indigenous lands.

Part III, Whose Future Increase?, reads seven primary legal documents, which yield 21 observations, from North Carolina, Delaware, Kentucky and Virginia. It separates four juridical operations: the child’s status at birth, title to an already born child, claims on issue not yet conceived, and later manumission. The cases show reproduction organized as property, and they also show disputed and variable outcomes, including a successful freedom claim.

Part IV, Transatlantic Population Circuits, examines how European institutions sent people, for example through British convict transportation, redemptioner debt and the 1880 Gersau emigration subsidy. It examines how American institutions received them through labor, land and citizenship rules, and how American models returned to Europe. Nineteenth-century U.S. growth came mostly from natural increase. Net migration accounted for 11.9% of national growth in 1790–1800 and 32.3% in 1850–1860. Those shares do not measure white-specific replacement.

What the parts can and cannot establish together

The parts use different kinds of evidence: documentary-conceptual, quantitative, legal-case and archival-comparative. Their findings converge on one institutional pattern, but this study does not combine them into a single causal law. A shared word such as increase does not license that inference. The Conclusion states what the convergence shows, and it lists the cohort, household and policy-linked evidence that the stronger hypothesis would require.

Part I. The Future White: Selective Reproductive Ideals and the Continuity of Settler Rule

Abstract

The expression white increase appears to describe a population growing through births and migration, but settler institutions have also used it to defend a restricted ideal of legitimate reproduction and a political order capable of surviving changes in the people designated white. How can a regime invoke the survival of a population while rendering some of its existing members unfit to reproduce that population? This article distinguishes three operations: numerical increase among a historically classified people; the attempted production of an ideal future group through selective eligibility; and reproduction of property and territorial rule. A focused documentary comparison of Benjamin Franklin’s 1751 population arithmetic, early twentieth-century race-suicide and eugenic pronatalism, and Virginia’s racial-integrity legislation of 1924 and subsequent revision efforts demonstrates that numerical increase and purity were neither identical nor uniformly prioritized. Virginia’s ancestry exception, defended and subsequently widened to protect favored social positions, is particularly revealing: proclaimed purity could be relaxed when rigid application endangered the standing of influential people. Read with recent histories of settler sociology and of racialized reproductive expropriation, the findings support a model of conditional selective futurity, not a prediction of white biological disappearance. The paper specifies what additional cohort and institutional evidence would be necessary to test the stronger conjecture that an imagined ideal is pursued at the expense of the existing population’s reproductive continuity.

Keywords: settler colonialism; racial classification; social reproduction; eugenics; whiteness; reproductive futurity; migration; racial-integrity law.

1. A population that is not yet the imagined population

The white settler has sometimes been presented as a present proprietor, an heir, and the bearer of a demographic future. Each description depends upon a different operation. A proprietor obtains a protected claim through law; an heir is recognized within a kinship and succession regime; a demographic future is produced through birth, survival, marriage, migration, and care. Yet these processes have frequently been represented under a single name: the increase or preservation of the white population. Conflating them prevents historical inquiry into situations where one operation contradicts another.

Consider a conditional proposition. Suppose an ideological regime claims that what really deserves to reproduce is not the present population classified white but an exceptional hereditary type that the population has not yet produced. The regime may treat actual people as inputs to a search for a future population whose founding conditions remain unfulfilled. Its promised reproductive future can then become a reason to exclude, sterilize, impoverish, deport, or dispossess people otherwise assigned white status. The strongest version predicts that the human material sustaining the search may be exhausted before its imagined success condition is achieved. This is a question about the revealed priorities and temporality of governing institutions, not a biological claim that whiteness denotes a natural human subspecies.

That formulation forces two distinctions. First, wanting fewer births among a politically stigmatized subset is not the same as actually driving a whole classified population toward disappearance. Second, preserving the rules of a settler state may require neither preserving every person recognized as white nor creating a more genetically ideal white person. The organization of land, labor, credit, household security, and citizenship may continue even when the population categories attached to those relations change.

This article interrogates the more modest and historically tractable proposition: when numerical reproduction and a selective ideal conflicted, which element did settler institutions preserve? It asks this across several specifically dated texts and laws. The study does not assume that a single actor called whiteness makes decisions, nor that its doctrines remain unchanged for centuries. The object is a set of institutions, claims, and decisions that can be compared without anthropomorphizing a racial category.

2. Literature, argument, and the difference from existing research

Patrick Wolfe’s account of settler colonialism as an enduring structure directs attention from initial conquest to mechanisms repeatedly securing the settler’s claim to replace Indigenous polities. That is crucial, but a state gaining territory, a household securing inheritance, and a population having children are not interchangeable outcomes (Wolfe 2006). Jennifer L. Morgan demonstrates that the very ability to turn kinship and childbirth into commodities was constitutive of racialized Atlantic capitalism (Morgan 2021). Hortense Spillers describes how the juridical form of hereditary slavery reorganized the language of kinship rather than simply denying enslaved people the capacity to make families (Spillers 1987). These studies caution against taking the census’s racial categories as self-evident biological groups.

Anna Stubblefield shows that eugenic judgments could define some ostensibly white people as tainted through intersections of disability, poverty, ethnicity, sex, and gendered moral expectations (Stubblefield 2007). Simone Caron traces how advocates of race suicide simultaneously promoted births by those judged fit and discouraged or prohibited births by those judged unfit (Caron 2008). Here the alleged preservation of a race already entailed differential reproductive value within the privileged category.

Two very recent contributions sharpen the need for a distinct claim. Musab Younis reconstructs demographic science in the history of settler rule and traces the reversal whereby dominant populations figure themselves as threatened by replacement (Younis 2026). Heidi Nicholls directly shows that early U.S. sociologists advanced both exclusionary purity and assimilationist/amalgamationist forms of eugenic social planning to stabilize the settler state (Nicholls 2026). It would therefore be neither novel nor defensible to announce simply that eugenics was colonial or that purity and amalgamation were competing colonial strategies.

The specific contribution attempted here is instead an inquiry into which objective is sacrificed when the demands are inconsistent. A purity ratchet is only one possible causal process. The same institution may relax its imagined standard to preserve the status of an established elite; it may broaden incorporation to stabilize settler claims; it may pursue hereditary exclusion while drawing labor from people denied membership; or it may be indifferent to the ability of ordinary classified whites to reproduce. A priority ordering inferred from decisions under pressure is a more discriminating object than an abstract assertion that every racial regime seeks an impossible final type.

3. Sources and method

The evidentiary design is qualitative comparative historical process tracing. The core documents are: (a) Franklin’s Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind (1751), sections 1–3 and 23–24; (b) Roosevelt’s letter to Charles Davenport of 3 January 1913 and Scott Nearing’s 1911 public debate over race suicide; (c) Virginia’s enacted Preservation of Racial Integrity (1924), including the specific Indigenous-ancestry exception; and (d) the subsequent Virginia legislative revision history as reconstructed by Virginia Humanities, checked against the exact wording of the 1924 statute. The comparison is purposive: each source exposes a tension between a demographic objective and a rule of privileged membership. This is not a probability sample of settlers or a representative census of eugenic policymakers.

Original full-text versions of Franklin’s essay, the Roosevelt letter, and the 1924 statute are available through scholarly or archival transcriptions. The present draft has checked the original wording reproduced on those surfaces. The fine-grained 1926 legislative chronology is taken from the scholarly historical synthesis published by Virginia Humanities and remains to be cross-checked against the House and Senate journals, original bill versions, and committee records before external submission. Nicholls’s 2026 article was read from the full open-access publisher text; relevant books are used at the level explicitly available through their publisher records and prior Sociology bibliography, without claiming close reading of unavailable chapters.

For each case, the analytical columns are: the stated future being protected; the population counted as entitled to that future; the eligibility criterion; the people whose reproduction or membership would be denied; the institution capable of enforcing the rule; and the observed revision when a rule conflicted with a valuable social position. A documented statement of ideology is evidence of an intention or aspiration, not evidence of an enacted policy or resulting demographic outcome. That restriction is central to the method.

Three rival explanations are retained. Numerical natalism predicts priority for increasing actual white-classified births. Uncompromising purity selection predicts persistence or tightening of ancestry standards even when large numbers of otherwise privileged people lose membership. Institutional status preservation predicts revision, exception, or selective accommodation when purity threatens groups whose recognized position the governing order depends upon. These are ideal-type predictions; actual laws may express more than one.

4. Franklin: the arithmetic of settlement and the limits of counting

Franklin’s 1751 essay begins by distinguishing demographic patterns in saturated towns from those in lands where household formation is easier. The available means of subsistence, the cost of forming a family, and opportunities to marry are central determinants of natural increase in the essay’s opening reasoning. Franklin also says some cities do not naturally replace their residents because deaths exceed births. This insight already complicates any attempt to treat the reproduction of a polity as equivalent to births within each of its settlements. Towns may persist through immigration even when local vital rates are insufficient.

But in the same essay Franklin describes colonial settlement as a means of expanding a preferred population, then narrows the meaning of white to a select group of Europeans. In sections 23–24, the wish to increase those he regards as white exists alongside hostility toward immigrants from many other European populations and toward the importation of Africans. His category is strikingly narrower than later official or popular versions of whiteness. Thus a policy can simultaneously favor more settlers, oppose particular sources of immigration, value greater births, and refuse to count substantial populations as desirable contributors to the racial future.

The significant tension is not that Franklin openly proposed extinguishing the population he favored. He did not. It is that his increase was selective from its earliest expression. The target of expansion was an institutionally chosen kind of population, not every person later to be described as white. The surrounding population could expand even as the imagined deserving subset remained selectively drawn. Franklin’s model of growth depends on available land and livelihoods, and the text’s confidence in colonial expansion presupposes the settler’s territorial claim. The Indigenous inhabitants whose political relations were displaced by that claim are not interchangeable with omitted census observations.

The demographic lesson must remain bounded. Nineteenth-century U.S. growth was largely the result of natural increase; Haines (1994) estimates very high U.S. fertility around 1800, and Haines’s historical components series assigns most growth in 1790–1860 to births exceeding deaths, rather than net international migration. Franklin’s statement about particular towns cannot be converted into a claim that the whole country, or all its white-classified population, was incapable of replacing itself without migration. Instead the essay supplies an early analytic distinction between local reproduction, migration-supported settlement, the distribution of land and household resources, and which settlers an elite wished to count.

5. Race suicide: an ideal with two incompatible directives

By the early twentieth century, the rhetoric of race suicide announced demographic crisis in a different register. Roosevelt’s 1913 letter to eugenicist Charles Davenport condemns the reproduction of people he calls defective while demanding more children from citizens he deems properly suited to reproduce. The livestock analogy in the letter is deliberate; the imagined future depends upon distinguishing acceptable and unacceptable parenthood. A recognized citizen is no longer necessarily a desirable reproducer.

The empirical meaning is more exact than “white supremacists wanted fewer white people.” Roosevelt sought more births among the favored group and fewer among those he stigmatized. The question is whether the exclusion rule would have the effect of reducing the size or viability of the very population the institution said it wished to maintain. An answer requires the size, age structure, and fertility of the excluded subgroup, the effects of imposed policy relative to available alternatives, and countervailing natalist interventions. A letter cannot establish any of those effects.

Scott Nearing’s 1911 essay intervened against the prevailing population alarm by distinguishing the sheer number of children from the supposedly improved quality of the population. The merits of that alleged quality are not granted here. The relevance is that historical contemporaries could themselves articulate a conflict between maximizing numbers and selecting a favored future. The contestation was therefore not an accidental contradiction discovered only after the fact; it was an explicit argumentative divide.

Caron’s history of race-suicide and contraception and Stubblefield’s analysis of “tainted whiteness” document the social conditions through which such a divide could become action. A purportedly white woman might be disqualified from bearing a legitimate reproductive future because of poverty, disability, ethnicity, perceived sexual misconduct, or institutional classification. The political category thus contains an internal frontier. But the frontier is not necessarily a mechanical ratchet: the state may encourage more births among an elite or change the categories themselves. The causal question is not simply whether exclusion happened but how much reproduction and freedom it suppressed, at whose initiative, and in defense of what institutionally valuable outcome.

6. Virginia: purity, exception, and the preservation of elite identity

Virginia’s 1924 Preservation of Racial Integrity offers a more discriminating test than retrospective inferences from sterilization totals. The enacted text prohibited a person classified white from marrying anyone except someone classified white, with a defined exception for people said to have up to one-sixteenth American Indian ancestry and no other non-Caucasian ancestry. In another provision, the state simultaneously claimed to be establishing a categorical racial standard without mixture. The political rule was therefore inconsistent with a simple literal instruction never to admit a trace of nonwhite ancestry.

The inconsistency was not a merely statistical flaw. Virginia Humanities documents debate around the exception, including concern among established white Virginians claiming descent from Pocahontas. When eligibility rules threatened elite reputations and family status, the rules were revised to accommodate those claims. A 1926 proposal to remove the exception was subsequently amended in the House to permit up to one-eighth American Indian ancestry, broader than the one-sixteenth exception in the 1924 law (Encyclopedia Virginia, “Racial Integrity Laws, 1924–1930”). The historical record of the 1926 proposal and amendments must not be confused with the exact final effect of every subsequent enacted statute; those are distinct legislative states requiring a complete bill history.

This case sharply differentiates purity as an absolute reproductive endpoint from purity as a negotiable legal technology of belonging. When the rule threatened to disqualify valued white identities, a social coalition could preserve those identities by rewriting the rule. The imagined standard was sacrificed—or selectively suspended—to preserve an already established elite. That does not make the practice benign: restrictions on Black and Indigenous familial recognition and the violent bureaucratic erasure of Indigenous identities persisted. The exception protected one group’s inclusion within whiteness while the system continued dispossessing and misclassifying others.

There is consequently a counterexample to an unqualified claim of monotonically intensifying purity. At least some influential participants had both the leverage and incentive to broaden a boundary that endangered their standing. The material object reproduced was their membership and the credibility of inherited superiority, not numerical equality for all residents or consistent application of a genetic principle.

This observation does not prove that reproductive selection generally failed, nor that elites always protected all people classified white. It shows why a serious theory of white self-consumption must specify whose reproduction is sacrificed and which privileges are preserved. A state may make ordinary people expendable while preserving a narrowly situated class. The alternative hypothesis of elite-status reproduction can thus account for behavior otherwise misread as a desperate attempt to create an impossible biologically pure population.

The 1930 distinction: protecting elite white ancestry while conditionally recognizing Indigenous life

The failed 1926 revision was not the end of the legislative history. Virginia enacted a different racial-classification statute in March 1930. Its original §67, printed in the Code of Virginia as Amended to Adjournment of General Assembly 1930, designated as “colored” any person with ascertainable African ancestry, and treated American Indian status through a complicated ancestry rule. It contained a limited exemption for people recognized as members of tribes residing on reservations allotted by the Commonwealth, provided they met state-defined blood-fraction criteria and remained domiciled there. This was an enacted rule, unlike the broadening to one-eighth Indigenous ancestry considered and indefinitely postponed by the Senate in 1926 (Virginia General Assembly 1930; Virginia Humanities 2020).

That distinction changes the question of whose reproduction and continuity the state claimed to protect. For established elite Virginia families, distant connections to Pocahontas could be preserved as an exception within whiteness. For actual Indigenous people, continued recognition could be made conditional on location and on a bureaucratic fiction about ancestry; the rule threatened even members of the same nation who lived off the named reservations. The law was not an ethnographic discovery about Indigenous people, a scientifically valid account of ancestry, or an act creating their nationhood. It was a tool of colonial governance that tried to subordinate Indigenous recognition to the state’s racial and territorial administration.

The view of Pamunkey chief George Major Cook, who publicly opposed this legislation, supplies a necessary counterposition to a history told only through white legislators. Cook had contested state registrar Walter Plecker’s reclassification program throughout the 1920s. At a February 1930 legislative hearing he condemned a proposal that would recognize only reservation-domiciled tribal members, leaving Pamunkey and Mattaponi citizens living elsewhere vulnerable to legal erasure. Watkinson’s historical biography reproduces his argument that Virginia had seized land, forests and fisheries and was attempting to take the people’s very name (Watkinson 2020/2026). The original committee hearing minutes and Cook’s correspondence have not yet been independently checked; the present evidence is a scholarly biography quoting a political intervention, not an independently transcribed original speech.

The asymmetry is revealing for the theory of selective reproductive futurity. It is not accurate to say the state simply pursued an increasingly strict purity rule applied equally to all residents. It negotiated elite white identity, sought to erase Black and Indigenous boundaries of belonging in different ways, and reallocated recognition by legal and territorial criteria. The social reproduction of settler power could be secured by controlling who counted, without any demonstration that white-classified people were numerically reproducing less. The state’s success condition was neither a coherent genetic criterion nor the biological viability of some final reproductive pair; it involved preserving its own authority to classify, exclude, recognize, and inherit. The enactments support that institutional reading, but the originating hypothesis that real white population continuity was consumed before a deferred ideal could be realized still requires demographic and causal evidence.

7. Settler sociology: purity and assimilation are competing solutions

Nicholls’s examination of early U.S. sociologists provides a further negative control. The racial projects of Earnest Sevier Cox and Robert Park did not pursue a single progressively narrower boundary. In Nicholls’s reconstruction, Cox defended segregation, expulsion, and racial purity as necessary to imperial stability, while Park and associates considered assimilation and amalgamation ways to secure loyalty and order. Their positions were inconsistent concerning who should reproduce and join the polity, yet compatible in naturalizing the settler state’s authority over Indigenous lands (Nicholls 2026).

The significance is institutional, not a claim that Cox and Park had identical intentions. If the desired end were simply the preservation of a biologically isolated white population, the assimilationist approach would be unintelligible. If the shared end were stabilization of the settler state, distinct policies could become rival means toward that end. The position of actual inhabitants relative to state categories could change without equivalent changes in births, deaths or genetic ancestry.

This is the point at which the originating hypothesis’s limiting image acquires its strongest historical use. The pursuit of a future supposedly worthy of reproduction can be indefinitely deferred or redefined because the political institution need not realize that future in order to continue. Its day-to-day reproduction may instead depend on property law, bureaucracy, territorial rule, extracted labor, and the replacement of one population classification by another. A claim that the ideal will eventually be embodied need not ever become a measurable production target for the institution to benefit politically from it.

8. Captive increase: reproduction of property without reproduction of inclusion

This does not mean white settlers’ own births ceased to matter. Indeed, the historical evidence contradicts a generalized claim of nineteenth-century national demographic incapacity. The more profound discontinuity is that one population’s reproduction could generate legal wealth and governing power for others without granting corresponding membership.

The 1662 Virginia rule of hereditary enslavement assigned a child’s legal condition through the mother. It permitted the enslaver’s property claim to extend into descendants; later slave law could capitalize the future “increase” of enslaved women as a transferable interest. Morgan (2021), Spillers (1987), and Alphonso’s explicitly non-peer-reviewed 2023 paper approach the relation among reproduction, kinship and value from different historical and conceptual directions. Their distinctions matter. The legal expropriation of reproduction is not evidence that every pregnancy resulted from a deliberately organized “breeding farm.” Enslaved people sustained family relationships and actively resisted the regime; neither the statistical observation of births nor the ownership claim exhausts the meaning of those lives.

Within the slaveholding order, a child’s birth could increase an owner’s expected labor resources and property claims even though the child lacked the civil status associated with white settler descent. Consequently a historical inquiry restricted to births counted as white would miss a central mechanism sustaining the social order claiming to represent white settlers. Conversely, counting births into slavery as if they proved a purposive program of manufacturing white “super specimens” would confuse separate mechanisms and historical periods.

A companion demographic research note uses Hacker’s historical estimates of births into slavery and Haines–Sutch’s national-birth reconstructions to calculate the proportion of counted births entering the system of hereditary bondage in 1790–1849 and 1790–1859. Those figures measure the scale of the captive reproductive generation, not the causal contribution of those births to every form of settler wealth or political power. An eventual historical-economic study would have to connect particular births and property claims to probate, price, collateral, succession, and institutional representation. The conceptual contribution here is the nonidentity of reproducing people, classifying legitimate descendants, and reproducing colonial property and rule.

9. What the original limit case would require as an empirical claim

We can now specify why the proposition that the system would consume every ordinary white person before finding two ideal progenitors is not yet an established historical finding. Let the first clock denote the amount of time required for an institution to reach its own, historically specific criterion for a legitimate reproducing founder set. Let the second clock denote the remaining supported reproductive continuity of an actual defined population or cohort. A claim that the second clock runs out first is meaningful only if both conditions have observable criteria.

A retrospective researcher must therefore recover explicit eligibility standards and their historical revisions; identify real feasible policy alternatives; observe which subsets bore the cost of rejected parenthood, impoverishment, mortality, displacement, or coercive treatment; and calculate the relevant cohorts’ continued childbearing and survival. Racial category changes and migration may alter the size of the counted population without any corresponding change in biological reproduction. Without these observations, predicting disappearance is a rhetorical extrapolation.

Moreover, the institutions are heterogeneous. A court protecting an old family’s “white” identity may relax a rule, a reproductive health bureaucracy may intensify exclusion, and employers may degrade the living conditions of ordinary white workers for reasons of accumulation not reducible to eugenics. If these processes simply coexist, treating them as the intentional acts of one selecting entity is an invalid causal inference. Evidence of shared finance, authority, ideology, and decision-making is required to say they form a common operational system.

Three propositions have different evidence statuses:

Observed historically: settlers and state actors sought population growth while specifying who was entitled to reproduce their political-racial future; exclusionary and assimilationist policies both served state continuity; the strictness of a legal whiteness rule could be relaxed for privileged people.

Mechanistically plausible: some institutions valued the reproduction of status, property and authority more than the continuity of all persons assigned white status; other people’s reproductive labor could materially sustain that order.

Not established: a self-reinforcing purity search that drove any entire white-classified population to demographic extinction before achieving a concrete hereditary ideal.

A theory that cannot distinguish these claims will either flatten the originating hypothesis into trivial eugenic exclusion or present a dramatic outcome it has not demonstrated.

10. Conclusion

The central problem of white increase is not necessarily a contradiction between a group’s birthrate and an externally imposed population. It is a contradiction among projects that call themselves the reproduction of whiteness. Franklin’s narrowly defined preferred settlers, the early twentieth-century distinction between proper and improper reproducers, and Virginia’s protection of elite membership through an ancestry exception show that numerical population, legitimate reproductive futures and institutional status have never been synonymous. Nicholls’s account of both exclusionary and assimilationist settler sociology shows that a still broader objective—the continuation of colonial rule—can survive competing reproductive prescriptions.

The evidence presently supports an institutional rather than biological conclusion. The future presented as worthy of reproduction may be repeatedly redefined while the existing people are governed, classified, exploited or deprived in its name. The strongest version, that ordinary reproductive continuity is exhausted before the alleged ideal is ever produced, remains an explicit hypothesis requiring measured cohorts and policy-linked counterfactuals. It should not be mistaken for an inevitable demographic forecast.

This shifts the research question away from who will count as the last white descendants and toward who has the authority to defer the promised future, who is made to bear the costs of that deferral, and which institutions successfully reproduce themselves without delivering what their reproductive ideology promises.

Part II. Birth into Captivity and the Reproductive Generation: Accounting for U.S. Births, 1790–1859

Abstract

How many births within the historical United States occurred under hereditary enslavement, and what is the proper denominator for expressing that contribution to a society’s reproductive generation? This research note reconstructs shares of all estimated national births occurring into slavery between 1790 and 1859. The numerator is J. David Hacker’s (2020) modeled decennial count of births into slavery; the denominator for 1800–1859 is the annual number of national births in the Historical Statistics of the United States reconstruction by Michael Haines and Richard Sutch. Because the latter series does not report annual births for 1790–1799, an explicitly assumed crude birth rate of 55 per thousand is applied to Haines’s mean mid-decade population of 4.520 million, with a 45–65 per-thousand sensitivity analysis. The resulting estimates are 17.20% in 1800–1849, 17.13% in 1790–1849, and 16.82% in 1790–1859. The last two point estimates vary within 16.89–17.37% and 16.65–16.99%, respectively, over the assumed 1790s birth-rate range; these are not confidence intervals for overall historical uncertainty. The study distinguishes births from population stocks and net migration, the historical census’s exclusions from a census of all births on Indigenous territories, and heredity of enslaved status from deliberate direction of particular conceptions. The estimated share identifies the scale of an expropriated reproductive generation. It does not by itself estimate maternal coercion, institutional profit from those births, or the causal dependence of white settler households on enslaved people’s reproduction.

Keywords: historical demography; slavery; fertility; births; social reproduction; family history; early United States; population measurement.

1. Introduction: a question of what the polity reproduced

A birth within a state cannot be assumed to reproduce membership in that state’s political community. In nineteenth-century United States slavery, the child’s juridical condition generally followed the enslaved mother. A birth could therefore reproduce an enslaver’s property claim, a household’s compelled labor resources, and a legally subordinated line of descendants rather than the liberty and equal civic standing of the newborn. Hortense Spillers’s (1987) study of kinship grammar and Jennifer L. Morgan’s (2021) study of reproduction within Atlantic slavery explain why this is not an abstract statistical curiosity. The legal and economic treatment of birth was part of the social order itself.

A related empirical question is the share of the state’s total reproductive generation entering slavery. Popular presentations have sometimes treated the striking magnitude of the institution as an estimate that one-quarter of all United States births in a particular early-national period were births into captivity. Such a number is only useful if the geographic and legal population, interval, numerator, and denominator are identical or their differences are explicitly reconstructed. For 1790–1850, the historical United States did not possess national complete civil birth registration. Both enslaved-birth totals and all-birth totals used here are modeled estimates. This note does not pretend to discover individual mothers in contemporary birth certificates; it calculates a precisely reproducible ratio of existing historical reconstructions.

The analytical claim is deliberately bounded. A substantial proportion of modeled births among the population counted as the United States entered life in a hereditary system of enslavement. Its percentage does not measure which pregnancies were coerced or assigned a purpose by owners, and it does not automatically identify the relative share of social production undertaken by enslaved families. Nor can the ratio settle whether populations classified as white relied predominantly on immigration to reproduce themselves. Those are different units of observation, with different comparisons required.

This distinction makes the accounting both historically serious and useful. The denominator is not simply a neutral national container. Early federal censuses excluded many Indigenous residents and shifted territorial coverage as the United States expanded. A national share of births within the population its demographers retrospectively count is not a share of all human births on lands now treated as the national territory. The state’s definition of a population is itself part of the historical process requiring explanation.

2. Sources, temporal units, and source-critical method

2.1 Captive-birth numerator

J. David Hacker’s article, “From ‘20. and odd’ to 10 million: The growth of the slave population in the United States” (Slavery & Abolition, 2020), estimates the total number of people who ever lived under slavery in the United States, using decennial population counts, historical enslaved-population growth, birth-rate assumptions, and import estimates. Table 1, column 7, reports the number of births into slavery by decade. For 1790–1859 the series is:

1790s 403,648; 1800s 583,395; 1810s 759,768; 1820s 1,003,859; 1830s 1,280,680; 1840s 1,539,820; 1850s 1,865,898.

The distinction between Table 1 column 7 (births) and the next column (people imported), or the later column (combined births and imports), is essential. A denominator of all births cannot correctly be paired with a numerator that includes imported persons. Neither the decennial growth of the stock of enslaved persons nor the total number ever enslaved measures births in a particular decade.

Hacker’s methodology involves assumptions about fertility and statistical coverage, including adjustments for slave populations in territories not enumerated in certain federal censuses. The numerator is therefore not exact to the reported unit despite being presented as an integer. The historical uncertainty around these estimates must be retained; the precision displayed here is arithmetic reproduction of a cited series, not evidence that we know the actual number of individual births exactly.

2.2 National-birth denominator

The Historical Statistics of the United States: Millennial Edition national demographic series, prepared by Michael R. Haines and Richard Sutch (Table Aa9–Aa14), gives estimated annual U.S. births beginning in 1800. The values are published in thousands. Summing the relevant years, rather than using the total change of the population, gives:

1800s 3,332,000; 1810s 4,375,000; 1820s 5,753,000; 1830s 7,483,000; 1840s 9,095,000; 1850s 11,694,000.

Summing the national birth column for 1800–1849 gives 30,038,000. Summing through 1859 gives 41,732,000. These values include births across the enumerated/reconstructed U.S. population, not just births among free people, people classified white, women legally married, or persons living in regions participating in slavery. The inclusion of births to enslaved mothers in the total is what makes the numerator a subset of the denominator.

These are annual national estimates, not original individual birth registers. Their documentation and reconciliation to historical population stocks need independent replication before submission, especially because early nineteenth-century boundary changes and territories outside federal census enumeration complicate both sources.

2.3 The missing 1790s decade

Table Aa9–Aa14 supplies national population estimates in the 1790s but leaves annual births and deaths blank until 1800. Thus a historical 1790–1849 or 1790–1859 fraction cannot be computed from its printed birth column alone. Haines’s distinct Table Aa15–Aa21 reports a 1790–1800 mean mid-decade population of 4.520 million and an estimated natural increase rate of 26.49 per thousand, not an observed crude birth rate. It is an error to relabel natural increase as births.

For a transparent first approximation, set the 1790s crude birth rate to 55 per thousand per year. Then births in 1790–1799 are estimated as:

B1790s = 4, 520, 000 × 10 × (55/1000) = 2, 486, 000.

We vary this assumed rate to 45, 50, 55, 60, and 65 births per thousand per year. This is a scenario sensitivity exercise only. The 1790s birth series is not observed in either source; the sensitivity range does not capture uncertainty in Hacker’s captive-birth estimate, regional population disparities, enslaved and free fertility differentials, or imperfect enumeration.

2.4 Estimator and period convention

Let Sd denote estimated births into slavery in decade d from Hacker, and By denote reconstructed births among the national population in calendar year y from Haines–Sutch, summed by decade. For any collection of matched years I:

$$ \widehat p_I=\frac{\sum_{d\subset I}S_d}{\sum_{y\in I}B_y} $$

with the 1790s denominator replaced by the above assumption where I begins in 1790.

The quoted period 1790–1850 can mean births from 1790 through 1849, or include 1850. This study explicitly adopts half-open decennial intervals, so the period 1790–1849 contains 1790 through December 1849 and 1790–1859 includes all of the 1850s. Hacker’s 1850s numerator cannot be divided into annual 1850 births without a new assumption, so reporting an exact 1790-through-1850 ratio would imply unsupported precision. The proposed 1776–1860 claim is outside the present estimate; extending it backward requires harmonized colonial-era denominators and post-1776 frontier jurisdictions.

All divisions are calculated from raw source values in the companion archived data carrier: `data/records/2026-10-17-captive-birth-shares-source-series.json`. The Engineering CAIRN record reader accepts the source’s exact NI address and extracts selected values through a bounded JSON pointer. A source read reproduces the evidence carrier, not the correctness of its historical interpretation or the original authors’ estimators.

3.4 The immigration hypothesis is a separate stock–flow problem

The originating question about settler social reproduction also asks whether colonial or postcolonial populations reproduce themselves locally or predominantly persist through immigration. The national series does not support a generalized inference of immigrant-dependent nineteenth-century U.S. growth. Haines’s Table Aa15–Aa21 records migration’s contributions to overall national population growth as 11.9% for 1790–1800, 13.5% for 1800–1810, 13.7% for 1810–1820, 7.1% for 1820–1830, 16.9% for 1830–1840, 27.0% for 1840–1850, and 32.3% for 1850–1860. Every decade of this period shows growth predominantly from births exceeding deaths. Population-scale stability through natural increase is therefore evidenced at the national level.

But these percentages are ratios of components of net growth, not ratios of births and not race-specific estimates. The descendants of immigrants may subsequently be born locally and count toward natural increase. In contrast, a particular mining town, plantation frontier, urban labor market, marriage cohort, denominational community, or racially restricted group might depend heavily on replenishment from outside. The national growth components cannot answer whether those subpopulations sustained themselves without migration. For each proposed local case one must collect linked vital events, age-sex structure and movement rather than substituting the national rate.

Thus two potentially true statements must be allowed to coexist: national nineteenth-century U.S. population growth was largely natural, while particular settler institutions could depend on imported labor, families and capital to maintain their local reproduction. The present birth numerator is even less capable of deciding a proposition about “white” locally born replacement because it was selected by enslaved legal condition, not by a stable ancestry classifier.

4. A birth’s membership and economic meaning

The denominator constructed by historical population accounting is a set of births occurring among the reconstructed residents of the state. It is not a sample of its authorized political members. Under hereditary slavery a child’s legal status often followed the enslaved mother, a rule famously established in colonial Virginia in 1662. This was an institutional operation turning kinship and procreation into the continuation of a legally captive line. Among its material consequences were claims to future compelled labor, sale, inheritance, and debt security (Morgan 2021; Spillers 1987; Alphonso 2023).

The relationship between property and the future is especially important. Alphonso’s preprint documents antebellum courts recognizing increase and future increase of enslaved women as distinct property interests. Its findings still require archival verification of the underlying cases before being treated as definitive, and the paper remains a non-peer-reviewed source at the date of this note. Nevertheless, the legal-historical question it raises is conceptually independent of a statistical birth share: a future person could be claimed as a contingent property interest before conception. The social order could attempt to reproduce value from prospective descendants without granting them property rights, liberty or political standing.

This suggests an important epistemic distinction. Biological events, legal effects of those events, and economic capitalization of possible future events are not equivalent. A birth count estimates the first; slave law structures the second; probate, credit, insurance, plantation books and court records must establish the third. A ratio of national births cannot be mechanically converted into an estimate of material contribution to capitalism or the governing state without evidence linking events to those financial and institutional relations.

Nor can it be equated with the prevalence of organized breeding plantations. The law’s confiscation of reproductive futures operated across plantations and households regardless of whether individual pregnancies were arranged by an enslaver. Enslaved people formed kinship relations, maintained care networks, contested their captivity and sometimes resisted sexual coercion. A defensible economic history must account for those lives as people acting under violence, not as a biologically self-propelled factory whose sole output was labor.

5. The limits imposed by the state’s population boundary

The term births in the United States sounds geographical, but historical population boundaries do not describe all human communities living on the lands that now fall within the state’s claim. Federal census law long distinguished Indigenous persons not taxed and excluded many Indigenous residents from the population counted for representation. Conquest and annexation changed which territories and peoples were included; legal conditions of free and enslaved persons changed in different regions over the period. Hacker explicitly discusses adjustments for some enslaved populations not enumerated in the earlier censuses. The denominator used in this paper inherits the reconstruction’s historical geographic and legal framing.

Therefore the fraction computed here is a share of estimated births within the historically reconstructed national population, not the proportion of all babies born on North American land subsequently annexed by the United States. It cannot measure the reproductive vitality or losses of Indigenous nations. Such peoples are not an error term created by the federal census; their sovereignty and families precede the state’s statistical boundary. A proper comparative study of settler reproduction would place Indigenous demographic histories beside settler classification and legal control without treating the first as a subset of the second.

The same warning applies to the race label white. Census legal categories changed historically, and the socially privileged settler category could incorporate or exclude migrants and descendants through formal status changes. An increase of people classified white is consequently not always the same as an increase in a biologically isolated population—no such white species population is assumed. Reconstructing “white natural increase” requires source-specific classification rules and ideally linked individual-level records, not a demographic identity imposed retrospectively.

6. What follows and what does not

The central empirical finding is consequential even though it does not confirm the more dramatic proposed percentage: roughly one-sixth of national births, in these particular historical reconstructions, occurred into a legally captive condition during 1790–1859. Approximately 7.44 million modeled births into slavery in those seventy years help explain how deeply the institution extended into the generation-by-generation reproduction of society. The statistic is not merely a measure of persons employed as labor; it shows the assignment of bondage at birth was structurally important to the continuation of the United States.

The result does not independently establish the stronger causal proposition that the white settler population could not reproduce itself while relying on enslaved people’s births. The national population grew largely through natural increase, including births of enslaved people. To show displacement, one would need compare white households’ reproductive behavior to economic access to enslaved labor, age structure, wealth, and location, and estimate outcomes under plausible non-slaveholding conditions. Wanamaker (2014) offers an econometric attempt involving slaveholder fertility and child prices, but a household substitution mechanism cannot be promoted into a national claim of white extinction.

Similarly, the result does not resolve whether an ideological quest for allegedly ideal white progenitors was willing to spend the lives of existing white persons in its pursuit. That is the companion paper’s conceptual and archival problem. The two papers can be placed in dialogue because one shows an immense material substrate of expropriated reproduction and the other studies the variable objectives of white-racial social reproduction. But neither paper may claim to have causally proved the other from a shared word such as increase.

A more demanding historical-demographic research program would estimate, at the county or household level, three separate trajectories: local births and deaths among those classified settlers; imported adults and later locally born children of immigrants; and reproductive activities denied inclusion yet used to reproduce property and government. The linkage to particular institutions should be judged against source provenance and competing explanations, not against an expectation that the originating hypothesis must come out true.

7. Limitations, research ethics, and contribution

Five limitations make this note unsuitable as a population-level causal experiment. First, both numerator and denominator are reconstructed rather than directly enumerated births. Second, the 1790s birth denominator is explicitly assumed because the annual source records no births in that decade. Third, changing territorial boundaries and exclusion of Indigenous populations prevent global interpretation of “all births.” Fourth, modeled birth-to-slavery counts cannot distinguish owners’ arranged conceptions from births within freely formed or constrained relationships. Fifth, the fraction does not measure institutional accumulation, economic output, or demographically self-replacing settlers.

Nonetheless the estimate is fully transparent within the scope of its assumptions. An independent reader can recover the cited decennial totals and annual birth estimates, sum the chosen intervals, change the assumed 1790s rate, and compare with the reported figures. These properties make it an auditable historical-demography note rather than an unsupported “napkin math” assertion.

The contribution is therefore twofold: a specific, reproducible percentage identifying the scale of captive births within the statistical generation, and an explicit refusal to mistake that percentage for proof of a particular breeding policy, a racial group’s future, or the whole social reproduction of a settler state.

Part III. Whose Future Increase? Reproductive Property, Jurisdiction, and Freedom in Antebellum Slave Law

Abstract

A newborn’s inheritance of enslaved status under maternal-status law did not necessarily settle which enslaver claimed legal title to that child. This study compares four published judicial opinions from North Carolina, Delaware and Kentucky (1839–1849), a fifth Kentucky case available only through secondary citation, and two letters by Thomas Jefferson (1819–1820). Covington v. McEntire allocated four living children of Binah to a remainderman after a life tenancy; Nelson v. Nelson awarded three post-testator children of Leah to the beneficiary holding title to their mother; Smith v. Milman distinguished maternal status from ownership and granted the freedom petition of Stephen; and Johnson’s Administrator v. Johnson’s Heirs liberated Lytha but left her seven children, born before emancipation, enslaved. Judicial calculations assigned costs of maintaining mothers and children to the holders of future gains, while Jefferson contrasted women’s anticipated reproductive capital with men’s immediately expended labor. The resulting regime was not a single universal law of ownership and cannot be equated with proof that all pregnancies were deliberately compelled. The findings show juridical mechanisms by which anticipated children could be treated as property while rights of actual mothers and children remained restricted, disputed or, occasionally, vindicated through freedom petitions.

Introduction: biological birth, status, title, and futurity are different objects

What does a future birth produce under hereditary slavery? A person born to a mother held enslaved might legally inherit her captive condition. But the birth did not mechanically identify who owned the child. Wills divided the mother’s immediate services and her successors’ property rights; deeds attempted to convey unconceived future descendants; manumission instruments could change the mother’s status at one date without changing her children’s prior status. Courts then translated claims upon an entirely uncertain reproductive future into disputes over identifiable people.

That distinction matters to the study of the materiality of settler social reproduction. The state could treat children of an excluded people as increased labor supply, assignable inheritance, or legal assets, thereby reproducing the wealth of those recognized as entitled to rule. This does not mean each particular child was born as the result of a specialized enslaver-directed pairing, nor that the reproduction of white-classified households collapsed. It means the reproduction of slaveholding property was not identical to the reproduction of the slaveholding families’ own children. Making that relationship empirical requires distinguishing the operation of law from the biology and desires of the people subjected to it.

The closest historical literature already understands this. Jennifer L. Morgan’s Reckoning with Slavery (2021) connects reproduction, gender and racial capitalism; Hortense J. Spillers (1987) examines slavery’s violence toward kinship recognition. Gwendoline Alphonso (2023), in an unreviewed APSA preprint whose full text was read in this inquiry, analyzes “future increase” as a separate property interest and reports a keyword-sourced group of 212 legal cases from NexisUNI, nearly four-fifths between 1830 and 1860. That search has not been independently replicated, and its results are neither an exhaustive universe nor a prevalence estimate. The contribution attempted here is not to rediscover prospective issue as property. It is to recover, compare and qualify court rulings with different legal outcomes, including a freedom petition, and to make their specific claims reusable as independent source-located research observations.

Material, selection and method

The evidence is a purposive collection, not a random sample: four complete nineteenth-century reported decisions read in public report transcriptions; one reported decision for which only index and later citations were recovered; and two contemporary enslaver-authored letters. The four original opinions are Covington v. McEntire, 37 N.C. 316 (1842), Nelson v. Nelson, 41 N.C. 409 (1849), Smith v. Milman, 2 Del. 497 (1839), and Johnson’s Administrator v. Johnson’s Heirs, 47 Ky. 470 (1848). Banks’ Administrator v. Marksberry, 13 Ky. 275 (1823) is retained as a secondary reconstruction, not falsely represented as an independently read original.

For each case the dataset separates: captive people named; testator or claimants; whether the mother was enslaved when a child was born; legal question of status versus title; wording and temporal boundary of bequest; any specified living issue; amount and scope of any monetary claim; decision’s result; and source limitations. There are four primary court opinions, one secondary legal report and two letters, generating 21 narrowly stated observations. Named children are not counted as independent random samples. The corpus overrepresents disputes reaching appellate courts and published in law reporters and has severe archival asymmetry in whose speech is recorded.

Karin Wulf’s (2023) historical account independently warns against treating Virginia’s 1662 statute as a uniformly enacted legal rule across colonial British America. Maternal status could be implemented via statute in particular colonies, in other places via custom or court doctrines, and the later assignment of ownership was jurisdictionally contested. The distinctions tested here belong to historically specified law, not an imaginary biologically fixed race.

Finding I. North Carolina divided reproductive title across time

In Covington v. McEntire (37 N.C. 316, 317–320), William McEntire’s 1832 will held Binah in a life interest under his widow Rebecca and then assigned Binah to his daughter Rachel Covington. At Rebecca’s death in 1842, Binah had four children then living who had been born during the widow’s tenancy. Executor Prior McEntire claimed those children under the residuary estate. A proposed division discussed one named child, Catharine, and $55 as the plaintiffs’ share if that theory prevailed. The amount was an offered distribution component, not an observed sale price for a child.

Gaston, J., applying precedent of his North Carolina court, treated issue born during the life estate as part of the remainder gift unless the will specifically withheld that increase. He affirmed Rachel’s claim to Binah and the children. The court expressly rejected an inference that different, more elaborate wording concerning another enslaved woman, Nan, diminished the gift of Binah’s issue. The lawsuit thus reveals two attempted temporal divisions of ownership: current labor and its expected future human descendants.

The four living children are a fact about that litigation. They are not Binah’s complete birth history or proof that the claimants orchestrated conception. The court’s own framework treated persons’ relationships to one another as a dispute over the extent of other people’s testamentary gifts. Binah’s account of her family is absent.

The court in Nelson v. Nelson (41 N.C. 409, especially 410–412, 416–418) dealt with a different textual problem. James Nelson’s 1843 will gave Leah and baby Anderson to Elizabeth Nelson, but another clause proposed dividing any additional increase of Leah among three daughters. After the testator’s January 1844 death, three more children were born to Leah. Ruffin, C.J., held that the separate future-increase gift concerned possible births before the testator’s death; the three post-death children passed to Elizabeth under the original bequest.

The court also reported $329.75 in costs of maintaining Leah and her family, requiring maintenance expenses to be borne alongside legal claims to associated income or future issue. That amount was not solely maternity costs, not a sale price, and not a universal price of childrearing. The legal argument nevertheless demonstrates the treatment of future persons as an expected return to a property owner for expenditures on a woman’s pregnancy and her children’s survival. Neither the legal category “increase” nor the ledger captures Leah’s own reproductive choices.

Finding II. Delaware separated inherited status from ownership and recognized a freedom claim

In Smith v. Milman (2 Del. 497, 498–499), the petitioner Stephen, son of Hessy, sought freedom. A contested testamentary chain raised whether his ownership had passed with his mother in a life estate or to another person. The Delaware court distinguished a child’s inherited free-or-enslaved condition from the identity of the person claiming title. It criticized the assumption, used by courts elsewhere, that partus sequitur ventrem compelled title to children to follow whichever person held a remainder in their mother’s labor. The court’s alternative factual analyses left Stephen’s birth order uncertain but agreed that Ann Smith had the power to manumit him. The resulting order granted Stephen’s freedom.

This is an analytically consequential countercase, not a benign exception that makes the regime equitable. The court used the slaveholding property doctrine itself to recognize a person who could claim freedom through a valid manumission instrument. A uniform model predicting the same remainder-rule result in Delaware and North Carolina is falsified by the expressed reasoning of the original reports. Equally, a triumphalist story that the court stood for freedom over slavery would misstate its ownership premises.

The contrast should not be erased by selecting only property-consolidating decisions. The legal apparatus made children into contested assets, but where title claims failed and manumission applied, one person’s freedom could be recognized. The result proves the need to preserve both positive and negative judgments in any larger case corpus.

Finding III. Kentucky separated a mother’s emancipation from seven children’s bondage

Johnson’s Administrator v. Johnson’s Heirs (47 Ky. 470, 470–474) asks an even more painful question: what happens to children born while their mother is temporarily enslaved and anticipating eventual freedom? Job Johnson’s will held Lytha in a life estate with widow Margaret Johnson, providing that she should become free when Margaret died, or upon attaining 21 if the widow died earlier. Margaret lived past Lytha’s majority. Seven children were born to Lytha before Margaret died.

The court held Lytha free after Margaret’s death, but her children had been born while she was enslaved and hence remained enslaved. The court ruled the seven were claimable by Margaret’s estate rather than by Job Johnson’s heirs, because he had retained no remainder in Lytha. The children were not awarded to Lytha herself. This result requires separating at least three historical dates: a testator’s promise, a mother’s actual emancipation, and each child’s birth.

A deferred promise of freedom could thus coexist with continued slavery for the mother’s descendants. The case cannot establish that Lytha’s seven pregnancies were deliberately compelled. It does show the legal relation by which the children were separated from their mother’s resulting political condition despite kinship. The court did not treat her status on the date of litigation as retroactively altering their status at birth.

A fifth Kentucky decision, Banks’ Administrator v. Marksberry (13 Ky. 275, 1823), reportedly involved conveyance of the future increase of a woman called Pen independently of her current services. Alphonso (2023), pp. 31–32, interprets the case as recognition of an assignable right to future issue and quotes its comparison to future products of other property. The Delaware Smith opinion cites that earlier decision. The 1823 text itself remains unrecovered. Its substantive rule is therefore a hypothesis grounded in attributed secondary analysis until the full original report and deed are checked. That source-status boundary is an actual scientific result of the retrieval, not an inconvenience to hide.

Finding IV. Jefferson valued future children as capital while measuring current labor as expenditure

Two letters supply evidence of what a particular Virginia slaveholder believed the ownership system should accomplish. In a January 17, 1819 letter to overseer Joel Yancey, Thomas Jefferson described five reported infant deaths over four years, urged that enslaved mothers have time to care for their infants, and valued surviving descendants over near-term field labor. The five deaths are an assertion in Jefferson’s letter; independent registers and causes were not recovered. His expressed concern must not be equated with a rejection of their ownership or with a claim that enslaved families received adequate support.

In his June 30, 1820 letter to John Wayles Eppes, Jefferson proposed transferring 20 enslaved people of varied ages and sexes instead of 10 men during a discussion of estate debt and family provision. His explanation explicitly treated women’s prospective childbearing as a source of capital addition, unlike men’s labor which was consumed by operations. This is a direct first-person admission of how he valued potential offspring in estate allocation. It is not evidence that a sale occurred, that any woman conceived by his direction, or that he knew the true number of babies a woman would bear.

A slaveholder’s incentive to preserve captive infants is different from the capacity of their mothers to exercise free kinship. In this archive, infant survival mattered to the property owner because it promised a human asset. A family surviving or finding affection under that regime cannot be reduced to the owner’s calculation. The Sam and Louisa Everett testimony from 1936 illustrates documented directed pairing as a separate mechanism from speculative rights over future descendants and from an owner’s preference for infant survival. It has its own record, witness, and non-prevalence limitations.

Four juridical operations, not a single factory

The case evidence compels a typed distinction between four operations: (1) categorizing the child’s slave/free condition at birth; (2) allocating the title to an already born person; (3) assigning present title or financial expectations in issue not yet born; and (4) changing status through an act of manumission. The first may historically depend on a maternal-status rule; the second depends on the terms of ownership and inheritance; the third can exist without an actual future child; and the fourth can separate the fate of parent and child.

These relations should not be compressed into a single count. A person sold in the domestic slave trade is not thereby newly born; an owner writing “future issue” into a deed has not thereby produced a pregnancy; a newborn legally held enslaved was not necessarily conceived under explicit orders; and a child freed through a petition does not imply the enslaver who once claimed ownership intended to relinquish control. Each mechanism has different evidence and a different number that could responsibly be counted.

The originating hypothesis contains another distinction: a settler political-economic order can continue materially even where the nominally privileged population is not reproducing in the way its ideology promises. The present case study establishes how Black captive reproductive futures could become inputs to white enslaver property and intergenerational estate transfer. It cannot quantify how many white births that displaced or test whether an ideological search for two ideal white founders ever consumed the actually existing white population. That would require historical matched household fertility, natural increase, migration, class-specific family support and policy deliberations—not just the vocabulary of “increase.”

Source bias, alternative interpretations, and next research

The court cases are selected because they survive and illustrate disputes. Four appellate opinions are not a probability sample of thousands of estates and cannot estimate prevalence of any rule. Enslaved mothers and children mostly appear as objects of litigation. Stephen’s freedom petition is an unusually direct adverse claim within the court record, but still does not supply his full life history. A true case series must include original trial dockets, antebellum wills, freedom petitions, enslaved people’s narratives and estate inventories, with an explicit inclusion rule and geographical frame.

A doctrinal historian could argue these opinions were principally ordinary will-construction exercises. At the level of procedural holding, this is true; but ordinary property rules governing future children reveal the material institutionalization of claims on lives that did not yet exist. A demographic historian could argue that enslaved people’s own fertility and family relationships produced most births without owner’s deliberate intervention. This paper neither denies nor quantifies that possibility. These explanations are compatible with legal expropriation; they undermine any attempted inference that the courts alone prove population-wide directed breeding.

The small case corpus shows at least three incompatible title rules, but which variations result from jurisdiction, time, drafting, emancipation terms or procedural history has not been statistically identified. Future research must recover the full 1823 Banks opinion, collate printed law reporters against the electronic transcriptions (the Kentucky text contains OCR noise), find original wills and probate files, and use informed Black historical scholarship and survivor family records to recover agency outside the property record. For the originating hypothesis, this is a necessary legal-economic ingredient, not a substitute for empirical cohort and political-economy tests.

Conclusion

A court’s treatment of future increase reveals a specific problem of settler social reproduction: birth could produce a human being whose anticipated life was already partitioned among other people’s property claims. Those claims were nevertheless variable and disputed. The North Carolina decisions attached actual children to testamentary title; Delaware distinguished status from ownership and granted one petitioner freedom; Kentucky separated a mother’s later manumission from her children’s ongoing captivity. Jefferson, from the perspective of an enslaver, stated that future captive children added to his capital.

The most defensible conclusion is not that all birth into captivity was directed industrial breeding. It is that the legal reproduction of an enslaving property’s value could be organized around captive descendants while the descendants’ own families and rights remained subordinated. Such a regime could reproduce privileged settler property through the reproductive capacity of people excluded from its privileged political and kinship status.

Part IV. Exporting Subjects, Importing Settlers, Returning Models

Transatlantic population circuits and American settler reproduction, 1718–1935

Abstract

Did US settler institutions reproduce themselves less through a self-sustaining white-born population than through a transatlantic circuit that moved precarious European people into American labor, land, kinship, and citizenship regimes, then circulated American practices back to Europe? This study disaggregates three processes too often collapsed as “white increase”: population accounting (births, deaths, migration, and classification), institutional reproduction (land title, labor contracts, coerced childbirth, jurisdiction, inheritance, and privileged citizenship), and outward circulation of political/social models. Historical demographic estimates contradict the strongest national version of migrant replacement: the United States’ nineteenth-century growth was mainly natural increase. Yet primary records demonstrate purposive state and municipal engineering of movement. The British transportation system (1718–1775), the 1834 Poor Law’s assisted-emigration provision, municipal records from Gersau, Switzerland (1880–1881), the US Homestead Act (1862), and the federal Immigration Act (1864) reveal different, nonidentical arrangements for relocating people and converting them into colonial labor or settler property holders. Racialized eligibility selected which newcomers were incorporated into a favored reproductive future. Two demonstrable forms of outward circulation are French commissioned study of American penal institutions in 1831 and German attention to US racial legal precedents in the 1930s. The proposed contribution is a dual-screened population-and-model circuit: sending jurisdictions and receiving jurisdictions classified people differently, and institutional reproduction may persist even while racial demographic categories change. The paper states specific rival explanations and archival tests, without equating immigrant indenture with hereditary racial slavery, nationality with statelessness, or a cultural analogy with proven bilateral causation.

1. The originating proposition and what could falsify it

The opening problem concerns material social reproduction. A settler population is not reproduced solely by biological fertility: households require access to food, land, wages, housing, care, safety, kin networks, and political membership. A settler jurisdiction may maintain its territorial and political arrangements via new arrivals even if particular neighborhoods, labor forces, or settler lineages fail to replace themselves locally. Its membership category can expand or contract by legislation or social recognition.

The stronger original conjecture is testable: some settler societies, locales, or recognized lineages may depend on immigration for their persistence, perhaps because powerful European institutions generated economically precarious outmigrants whose relocation was useful to settler development. The further conjecture is recursive international feedback: after importing people, American institutions produced forms of culture, industrial management, penal organization, and racial legislation observed or appropriated in Europe. This is a proposed causal circuit, not a presumption of a single European or US policymaker’s integrated design.

The following claims must not be presumed: 1. That the national US population in the nineteenth century lacked natural increase. 2. That all European migrants were legally stateless, enslaved, deported, or subsidized. 3. That a single “feudal Europe” enacted a homogeneous bilateral settlement pact with an independent United States across colonial and modern periods. 4. That outward influence proves that America invented the influenced practice or that Europe accepted it uncritically. 5. That net immigration into the total population measures net white settler reproduction, or that those counted as immigrants all became landholding settlers. 6. That the presence of eugenic ideology proves its demographic effects, or that a discriminatory source’s allegations about migrants are true.

2. Units of analysis: distinguish six reproductions

Biological/population flow: Define a jurisdiction g, period t, and historically documented classification rule c. Then the stock’s change is births minus deaths plus entry minus exit plus changes due to territorial boundaries and classification. A racially classified subgroup additionally requires people entering or leaving that classification without moving or being born/dying. Children of migrants count as births where born, not as contemporaneous immigration, while descent must be analyzed by parental origin in a separate cohort dataset.

Household continuity: Formation, dissolution, marriage permission, wages, shelter, care work and heirs. Survival of a household is not identical to increase of aggregate heads.

Labor force continuity: Employers may replace labor via immigration, internal migration, wage coercion, contract debt, temporary employment, or captive birth. Do not assume that each laborer is or becomes a settler.

Territorial/jurisdictional continuity: Land claims, Indigenous dispossession, survey, title, treaties, military enforcement and jurisdiction. This dimension cannot be deduced from national population arithmetic.

Eligibility / the “future” population: Migration restrictions, naturalization, marriage laws, racial classification, eugenic exclusions and pronatalist preferences set differential membership conditions. The classification “white” is itself historically political and uneven; it is not a stable biological input variable.

Return flows: Remittances, return migration, commercial models, reports, letters, law, institutions, administrative techniques and cultural works transmitted toward Europe; not all return flows are “culture” and not every comparison is direct borrowing.

The paper complements—not displaces—the existing studies on selective futurity, births into captivity, and reproductive futures as property. These address independent dimensions and should not be forced into one causation estimate.

3. First result: national numerical replacement and institutional reproduction diverge

Michael R. Haines’s 1994 historical reconstruction estimates that the US population grew predominantly through natural increase between 1790 and 1920. The historical growth-accounting series reproduced by the National Academies attributes 11.9% of US population increase in 1790–1800, 32.3% in 1850–1860, and 35.3% in 1900–1910 to net international migration. The respective remainder came overwhelmingly from natural increase under this accounting. These are all-population national period estimates, not directly observed white-specific or local settler lineage survival figures.

The reverse generalization is also unsafe. Statistics Canada estimated that 97.6% of Canada’s 2023 national growth came from international migration (permanent plus non-permanent) and 2.4% from natural increase. Australia’s Bureau of Statistics reported for the year ending December 2024 net overseas migration of 340,800 and natural increase of 105,200, of approximately 445,900 total growth (rounded), or ~76.4% attributed to net overseas migration. This establishes that immigration-dominated national growth is possible in named later settler states/periods, not that these immigrants were white or all settlers or that nineteenth-century US growth had the same structure. In particular temporary residents must not be silently treated as permanent settler reproduction.

The claim to test thus becomes: where, when, among whom, and in what institutional function was the reproduction of settler rule dependent upon migrants even when national growth was mostly births? Counties, urban immigrant neighborhoods, railway construction, newly opened homesteading tracts, white-eligible citizenship groups and colonial labor systems may have different answers.

4. Sending people: four materially different mechanisms

4.1 British convict transportation (1718–1775): a colonial regime, not a US treaty

Britain’s 1718 legal and Treasury-backed convict transportation regime exported people convicted by its courts to British American colonies. A. Roger Ekirch estimates roughly 50,000 transported convicts, 1718–1775; the US Department of Labor’s labor-history account describes statutory seven- and fourteen-year service terms. Ekirch documents a Treasury contract in 1718 at £3 per person with a merchant; private traders purchased and marketed bound labor in the colonies. The transported people were compelled by British penal power, and American colonial masters exploited the resulting labor. They were not sent as part of a government-to-government deal with the United States, which did not exist as an independent republic in 1718. A term of convict service is not juridically identical to hereditary racial chattel slavery.

4.2 Redemptioners: transport debt transferred into American employment

Mid-eighteenth-century German migrant Gottlieb Mittelberger described a market at Philadelphia in which people unable to pay passage could not disembark until purchasers undertook to cover travel debts in exchange for service. His testimony is reproduced with editorial provenance by George Mason University’s History Matters project. This mechanism was merchant- and migrant-contract mediated, not automatically a European sovereign’s deportation order. It could expose migrants to harsh constraint, selection, and unequal bargaining positions. Farley Grubb’s peer-reviewed quantitative study of 4,455 German and British servant contracts, 1771–1804, finds mean servant compensation comparable with resident free-labor compensation in the sampled auctions, while recognizing restrictions and variance. That evidence is an important competing interpretation of the market, not proof that all conditions were benign or that Mittelberger fabricated his experience. Analyze legal and practical agency separately.

4.3 Parish, workhouse and landlord assistance: collective administration, not universal shipping

The English Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 allowed home parishes to fund emigration for poor residents. Britain’s National Archives identifies Poor Law Commission records, Poor Law Union files MH 12, correspondence MH 13/252 (1853–54), and MH 19/22 (1836–76) for direct reconstruction. Irish workhouses and landlords also organized or subsidized passage during the Great Famine; Hidetaka Hirota studies the changing population origins and their relation to US anti-Irish restriction.

Scale limits are indispensable. An English Historical Review assessment of Gerard Moran estimates that roughly 100,000 famine-era emigrants received even minor landlord subsidies, less than 3% of 1835–1855 outflow. The estimate of up to 300,000 assisted Irish departures to North America over the nineteenth century was dwarfed by approximately eight million total departures. Assistance happened, but the stronger thesis that most arrivals were institutionally shipped at official expense is contradicted for this population and interval. Gary Howells’s local study of three English counties also warns against simplifying all assistance as expelling people the sponsors considered worthless; some programs were articulated as reciprocal welfare and paternalistic aid.

4.4 Gersau, Switzerland (1880): a direct, unusually high-resolution sending decision

A municipal assembly of Gersau on 22 August 1880 adopted an emigration-subsidy program during unemployment. Its decision, municipal explanations, and US consular correspondence survive together in the US State Department’s published Foreign Relations documents dated 24 January 1881. This is a concrete research-grade case, unlike diffuse claims that “Europe” exported unwanted bodies. Directly recorded rules:

  • A Gersau town council assessed eligibility; applicants had to be judged physically and mentally sound, have a destination, and leave dependents supported.
  • Adult members could receive 250 francs; sons aged 10–18 100 francs, with separate lower rates for younger boys.
  • An applicant receiving aid relinquished the benefits of communal membership for twenty years, with provisions to repay the subsidy (and in some cases interest) if returning earlier and seeking restoration of those benefits.
  • The council made individual contracts and used agents for Lucerne–New York passages.
  • Gersau reported 29 male emigrants and 5,755 francs of assistance for the year 1880.
  • The council said it could end the program if local employment recovered.

This is an instance of selective externally financed relocation combined with a reduction of future municipal obligations, with explicit transport contracting. It does not mean these people became legally stateless; the broader 1855 Swiss Federal Council position reproduced in the diplomatic compilation expressly contemplated continuing Swiss nationality despite acquisition of another citizenship. Nor is the pre-1883 rule in itself evidence of a modern genetics/eugenics program: its fitness filter is documented, its intellectual lineage is not.

The US consular and diplomatic correspondents regarded some subsidized migration as undesirable and protested particular cases. A related July 1881 exchange confirms US diplomatic opposition to a Swiss commune assisting a named penitentiary inmate to come to America. Therefore this evidence points to strategic interaction and conflict among municipal, federal, private, and receiving actors, not a proved agreement to jointly empty European poorhouses.

The 1881 diplomatic review quotes a 19 March 1855 Swiss Federal Council letter to US Minister Fay, explaining municipal membership-based compensation and requesting favorable treatment of Swiss emigrants. It is evidence of government-to-government communication, but the 1855 original has not yet been independently collated, and the 1881 US commentator’s description of US concessions is a claim needing verification in receiving-side policies. The same diplomat analogized compelled movement to old European feudal practices; that analogy in a political complaint cannot establish that 1880 Switzerland was a feudal state.

5. Receiving people: selective labor, land, citizenship and territory

The Homestead Act of 20 May 1862 offered specified adult citizens and those formally declaring intent to become citizens entry into federally claimed land, ordinarily 160 acres, conditioned on residence, improvements, and later title. The National Park Service explicitly documents the law’s intended encouragement of European immigration. Its resource was land: an institutional mechanism to turn eligible migrants and other entrants into landholding settlers. The lands were not socially empty: Indigenous nations maintained political claims, families, and relations to them. The Park Service documents links between homesteading and Indigenous displacement; its summary traces reservation acreage from 138 million in 1887 to 48 million in 1934 in the context of allotment. Manu Karuka situates railway finance and Chinese labor together with Lakota, Cheyenne, and Pawnee jurisdiction and dispossession. Here one cannot use a white immigration account while omitting Indigenous territory and non-European labor.

The Act to Encourage Immigration, 4 July 1864 established federal immigration administration, regulated foreign-made labor agreements, and explicitly permitted qualifying arrivals to pledge up to twelve months’ wages against transport costs. The enacted text also expressly disclaimed authorization to create slavery or servitude. Thus the statute documents purposeful recruitment and monetization of migrant labor capacity but directly rebuts the literal legal inference that all such migrants were enslaved. Labor exploitation, debt dependency, or coercion must be demonstrated through contracts, enforcement and wages, rather than asserted from the existence of passage finance.

The 1790 naturalization rule, largely limiting naturalization to free white persons, and the 1870 extension to persons of African nativity/descent show that legal recognition as a future citizen was selectively produced. Chinese Exclusion (1882) demonstrates that some non-European workers whose labor could be used in an expanding economy were denied a comparable avenue of admission and citizenship. The Immigration Act of 1924 curtailed southern/eastern European arrivals, privileged particular earlier national-origin populations, and excluded Asian immigrants under its rules. These are historically separate policies, not a single consistent racial algorithm.

At the same time, the legal and social boundary of whiteness requires careful treatment. Noel Ignatiev’s history of Irish incorporation is influential, but Cybelle Fox and Thomas Guglielmo (2012) challenge overly simple claims that previously legally nonwhite Europeans merely “became white.” We must separately trace legal racial eligibility, social acceptance, privilege, local discrimination, and racial violence. The relevant working object is membership decisions under dated institutions, not an ahistorical count of a fixed white race.

6. Eugenics as the attempted control of imported generations

Eugenics is not an optional supplementary chapter to this inquiry. It makes explicit that people were understood by certain institutions not only as current workers and residents but as potential ancestors of future citizens. “Selective futurity” describes the conflict among current useful labor, preferred future ancestry, and the continued institutional authority to classify both.

Harry H. Laughlin’s testimony to Congress and his Eugenics Record Office networks helped press biological claims against newer immigrants. The 1924 Johnson–Reed quota framework used 1890-based national-origin distributions to favor selected national stocks. An immediately contemporary critical response exists: Joseph M. Gillman’s 1924 American Journal of Sociology article argued Laughlin’s datasets were incomplete and his statistical conclusion unfounded. The eugenic inference was disputed at the time; do not reproduce its population judgments as true. Heidi Nicholls (2026) demonstrates that the settler aims of early sociological policy could be advanced by apparently opposite eugenic strategies: uncompromising purity or assimilation/amalgamation. Thus immigrant inclusion and immigrant exclusion could both be mobilized in defense of settler jurisdiction.

A theoretically sharper question follows from joining sending-side Gersau to US immigration law: Was the institution trying to maximize actual settlers, maximize the population recognized as fit future ancestors, maximize extractable present labor, minimize receiving/public-relief costs, or secure territorial/legal continuity? Those aims differ and their relative priority can only be inferred through choices when they conflict.

The reproduction of enslaved African-descended people was governed through a materially distinct legal regime, with hereditary slave status, sexual violence, kinship deprivation, and property claims to children. Jennifer L. Morgan’s work documents that foundation. The separate Lenticule demographic manuscript reconstructs approximately 17.20% of recorded/modelled US births during 1800–1849 as births into legal captivity, conditional on specified sources and limitations. This work must not be merged with a story in which all European migrants were slaves: such conflation would erase specific Black reproductive expropriation and its intergenerational legality.

7. Returning social and governmental models to Europe: three tests

7.1 Tocqueville and Beaumont, 1831–1833: observation requested from Europe

Alexis de Tocqueville and Gustave de Beaumont entered the US in 1831 under a mission sanctioned by the French government to study penitentiaries, observed broader American political and social arrangements, and returned with material that informed an 1833 published comparative study and Tocqueville’s later Democracy in America. The book was not an official government report despite their government-sponsored mission, as the Cambridge historical treatment emphasizes. Yale University Press’s edition of Tocqueville’s letters identifies the governmental prison-study commission and the letters returned to France. Here Europeans explicitly sought American institutional experience as potential policy knowledge; the case is more precise than the metaphor of America sending culture. It shows an outbound observation and translation channel, not an American state program designed to export lessons.

7.2 Fordism: US industrial labor organization debated as a European model

Antonio Gramsci’s Americanism and Fordism analyzes attempts to import US industrial organization and the social disciplining of labor into an older European social structure. This is relevant because the export was not merely an artistic object: labor rhythms, managerial disciplines, household assumptions and mass consumer culture can operate as social-reproductive techniques. A historical reading must distinguish discussion of a model from established adoption of an identical institution. Contemporary scholarship on Gramsci’s treatment of industrial and reproductive discipline documents the distinction.

7.3 Germany and the US, 1920s–1930s: policy feedback via racial law and eugenic networks

A more direct record of backward legal influence is James Q. Whitman’s Hitler’s American Model (2017): Nazi legal planners took sustained interest in American restrictive citizenship and anti-miscegenation rules during the construction of their own 1935 Nuremberg Laws. Stefan Kühl’s The Nazi Connection (1994) documents transnational contacts among eugenicists and influence in both directions, rather than a simplistic American sole origin. Some American eugenicists also endorsed German policies. This is a documented appropriation of American legal precedent with identifiable channels, yet German political movements had their own antecedents and agendas. It is not evidence that migration from a specified village like Gersau caused those legal exports generations later.

8. Provisional model: a dual-screened transatlantic circuit

A parsimonious proposed sequence is:

European household insecurity or penal judgment → origin-side rule for assisted/free/coerced departure → merchant, state or family financing and Atlantic passage → receiving-side entry and eligibility determination → labor assignment and/or land-and-household reproduction → citizenship, inheritance and population classification → texts, administration, culture and policy models returned through transatlantic networks.

There need not be a single institutional operator spanning the entire chain. “Circuit” is appropriate if identified transmissions and feedback can be demonstrated for specific actors and time periods; it is otherwise a heuristic arrangement of separate events. The hypothesized dual screen comprises the origin’s departure eligibility and the destination’s entry/citizenship eligibility. The same person can be desirable for the origin to lose yet undesirable for the receiving state to admit. Gersau’s selection rules and the American consul’s protests show this tension at the level of documented institutions.

Distinct outcome measures: - `D_nat(g,t)`: share of jurisdictional population growth attributable to net migration, with births and deaths independently reported. Does not measure racial membership. - `D_lineage(g,c,t)`: cohort survival and descendant stocks for specified historical membership categories, with conversions and reclassifications separately recorded. - `D_local(g,t)`: fraction of new households or resident labor sourced through geographic inward migration, including internal US migration separately. - `D_property(g,t)`: observed changes in land title and control tied to migration, including Indigenous jurisdictional origin and source of survey/title. - `D_cost(o,t)`: sending locality relief expenses, travel subsidies and subsequent relief/pension claims before and after a funded departure, net of repayments. - `D_exploitation(g,t)`: actual wages, mobility restrictions, debt contracts, violence, legal status and exit rights. Never infer this from migrant identity alone. - `D_return(e,t)`: traceable citation, translation, personnel, legal borrowing, procurement, or organizational adoption from a specified American practice to a specified European receiver.

Comparative hypotheses and falsifiers: - H1, replacement at scale: Migration exceeded natural increase for the specified settler population, region or period. Falsified in the Haines national all-population accounting for nineteenth-century US periods; open for bounded subgroups and some modern countries. - H2, official population export: Sending bodies financially supported emigration because continuing local obligations exceeded relocation costs. Gersau provides plausibility via subsidy and twenty-year communal benefit surrender. Causal proof requires actual fiscal accounts, other expenditures, and testimonies; rival: genuine assistance to voluntary movers. - H3, immigrant conversion: Receiving institutions channeled migrants into landholding, employed, and legally favored settler roles. Requires linked household, land patent, employment and status records, not a Homestead statute alone; rivals: migrants entered cities, returned home, or were excluded. - H4, selection over numbers: Restrictive gatekeepers accepted economic loss or foregone population increase to maintain an ideal future group. Needs legislation, legislative trade-offs, and demographic cohorts; rival: labor/welfare, diplomatic or wartime reasons produced selection. - H5, outbound model: American practices measurably changed a named European institution. Tocqueville proves observation/reporting; Whitman documents influential legal borrowing; a generalized “Europe received American culture” remains unmeasured without specific transmissions. - H6, reproduced sovereignty rather than lineage: Settler territorial claims or economic institutions persisted despite turnover or reclassification of nominal beneficiaries. Needs administrative continuity, beneficiary turnover and Indigenous-source jurisdiction data; rival: existing households themselves reproduced most relevant relations locally.

Important negative evidence: British government transport before US independence cannot show US diplomatic cooperation; Irish official/landlord assistance represented a small minority of total migration; 1881 Swiss-US friction defeats a universal harmonious bilateral program; 1864 US law formally excluded slavery/servitude from its immigration-financing contract authority; historical national natural increase refutes the blanket claim of US demographic incapacity.

10. Conclusion

The literal conjecture that American national settlement survived chiefly because Europe continually supplied white replacements is too strong for the early US demographic evidence. But a different, demonstrably researched question survives: how did institutions on each side of the Atlantic move people who were costly or constrained in one jurisdiction into potentially useful labor, property, and future-parent categories in another, while selecting who was permitted to belong—and how did the receiving society become a source of transferable political and social techniques?

The contribution of this draft is a case-backed design rather than a rhetorical declaration. The archival record already supplies three distinct contrasts: British legally forced transportation, Swiss municipally financed and selectively eligible departure, and US state-assisted reception through labor/land rules. It also supplies two documented modes of outward observation and policy borrowing. No single historical period or collective racial “will” unifies them. A proper explanation must show which actors’ decisions, in which year, changed which person’s material ability to live, work, own land, maintain family relations, or belong to a polity—and which European institution demonstrably used the resulting knowledge. That is a researchable history of settler social reproduction as institutional, demographic, and intellectual circulation.

Conclusion: what settler institutions preserved

Across four kinds of evidence, the institutions examined here preserved the authority to classify people and to appropriate reproduction more reliably than they preserved the reproductive opportunities of every person they classified as white.

The convergent finding

Each part reaches this result through its own evidence, and each part’s result can stand without the others.

  • Classification bent to protect status (Part I, observed). Virginia’s purity rule carried an ancestry exception for favored genealogies. The legislature considered widening that exception in 1926, and in 1930 it enacted distinct rules. The standard that claimed to protect a race changed so that particular families remained inside it.
  • Reproduction was organized as property without membership (Part II, observed; Part III, observed). About one U.S. birth in six in 1800–1849 was a birth into hereditary slavery. Antebellum property law could assign title to children, and even to issue not yet conceived, while those children’s families and rights stayed subordinate. Settler property grew through the reproduction of people the settler polity excluded.
  • Eligibility narrowed within the classified population (Part I, observed from secondary scholarship and administrative records). Race-suicide pronatalism urged births among people judged fit, and eugenic policy discouraged or prevented births among people judged unfit. Some of the people judged unfit were themselves classified white.
  • Settler growth drew on institutions that selected migrants (Part IV, observed for named cases). Sending authorities financed, compelled or conditioned departure. Receiving authorities admitted, employed and settled people under eligibility rules, and their models were later studied and borrowed in Europe.

Taken together, these findings support a model of conditional selective futurity. In this model, the future described as worthy of reproduction can be redefined repeatedly, while existing people are classified, excluded or deprived in its name. Meanwhile property, jurisdiction and authority continue through other people’s reproduction. The status of this model is mechanistically plausible. It fits every part’s evidence, and two rival explanations remain open: routine accumulation that needed no reproductive ideal, and independent policies that coexisted without coordination.

What is not established

The stronger proposition remains not established. In that proposition, a self-reinforcing search for an ideal founding population exhausts the actual white-classified population before the ideal is reached. The evidence assembled here also contains counterevidence:

  • The white-classified census population of North Carolina grew from 1950 to 1960, during a period of state sterilization.
  • Nineteenth-century national growth came mostly from natural increase.
  • Purity standards were relaxed as well as tightened.

A test of the proposition would need four things:

  1. explicit historical eligibility standards and their revisions;
  2. feasible policy alternatives that decision-makers rejected;
  3. cohort-level fertility, survival and migration for defined populations, with reclassification recorded separately from birth and death; and
  4. evidence of shared finance, authority or decision-making. Without that evidence, policies that coexist cannot be treated as one selecting system.

The question this changes

The finding moves the research question. The question is no longer who will be the last descendants counted as white. It is who holds the authority to defer the promised future, who is made to bear the costs of that deferral, and which institutions reproduce themselves without delivering what their reproductive ideology promises.
