## Central thesis, evidence, and counterevidence

**Research thesis.** A settler racial order can preserve its authority to classify descendants, appropriate other people's reproductive labor and maintain property and jurisdiction while failing to protect the reproductive opportunities of all people currently classified white. The strongest image of the future ideal exhausting its population is a **conditional hypothesis**, not a demonstrated demographic extinction or evidence of a biological white species.

Four histories must stay distinct: numerical births and survivorship; changes to who counts as white; eligibility to produce a supposed preferred future; and reproduction of property, sovereignty, labor relations and institutional authority. The proposed original contribution is a **revealed-priority comparison**: under observable conflict, which of those futures is actually preserved and which is made expendable? Rival explanations—routine property accumulation, changing classification, elite-status preservation, and independent policies operating without coordination—remain open.

Numerical anchor: modeled births into hereditary slavery / reconstructed U.S. births = **5,167,522 / 30,038,000 = 17.20%** (1800–1849), **5,571,170 / 32,524,000 = 17.13%** (1790–1849), or **7,437,068 / 44,218,000 = 16.82%** (1790–1859). The 1790s denominator assumes a 55-per-thousand crude birth rate, not observed registration. These are **not** estimates of the prevalence of forced conception or the share of all births on Indigenous lands.

Nineteenth-century national U.S. growth was substantially natural increase; the recorded migration contribution of 11.9% in 1790–1800 and 32.3% in 1850–1860 does **not** measure white-specific replacement, temporary migrants, or local settlement. The 1926 Virginia House amendment expanding a white ancestry exemption **did not become law**; the enacted 1930 law and Indigenous challenges are separately examined. The legal case study distinguishes status at birth, title to a child, prospective issue, and an actual act of manumission.
